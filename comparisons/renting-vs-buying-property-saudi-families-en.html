Renting vs Buying in Saudi Arabia: The Family Decision Guide

Jun 30, 2026 12 min read

According to REGA, For every family in Saudi Arabia , whether Saudi citizens benefiting from the Sakani programme or expats building their lives on an iqama , the question of renting versus buying a home is one of the most consequential financial decisions they will ever make. Get it right, and you build generational wealth, stability, and a sanctuary that grows with your children. Get it wrong, and you could be locked into a mortgage that strains your budget, or throwing rent money into a black hole for years without building equity.

This guide breaks down the decision with current Saudi market data, real costs, Islamic financing options, and a clear framework for making the choice that fits your family's situation.

The State of the Saudi Property Market in 2026

Before comparing rent and buy, it is essential to understand where the Saudi housing market stands today. Under Vision 2030, the Kingdom has undergone a housing revolution. The Real Estate Development Fund (REDF), the Sakani programme, and massive infrastructure investments have reshaped the landscape of homeownership.

The Saudi government has set a target of 70% homeownership among citizens by 2030. As of early 2026, the rate stands at 63.2%, up from 47% in 2016 , a remarkable transformation driven by subsidised mortgages, Sakani grants, and increased housing supply.

For expats, the picture is different. While property ownership is legally possible, the combination of high down payment requirements (20-30%), limited financing options, and the temporary nature of most employment contracts means that fewer than 2% of expatriates own their homes. Yet this is changing: the Premium Residency programme and longer-term contracts under Vision 2030 are slowly shifting the calculus.

Renting in Saudi Arabia: Costs and Liberties

Renting is the default choice for the vast majority of families in Saudi Arabia , both Saudi and expat. For good reason: it offers flexibility, lower upfront costs, and fewer long-term commitments.

Advantages of Renting

  • Flexibility: You can relocate for a new job, better school district, or changing family needs without selling a property. For expats whose iqama is tied to an employer, this is the single most important factor.
  • Low upfront cost: Typically one year's rent paid in advance (sometimes negotiable in quarterly or monthly instalments), plus a 5% municipal fee. No 20-30% down payment needed.
  • No maintenance burden: Major repairs , plumbing, AC replacement, structural issues , are the landlord's responsibility. For a family budget, this predictability is valuable.
  • Access to premium compounds: Many of the best family compounds in Riyadh (Diplomatic Quarter, Al-Nakheel, Al-Malqa) are rental-only. Buying into these areas is either impossible or prohibitively expensive.
  • School district flexibility: If your child gets into a better school in a different neighbourhood, you can move. Homeowners must either commute or sell.

Disadvantages of Renting

  • Zero equity: Every riyal of rent is gone forever. Over 10 years of renting a 90,000 SAR/year villa, that is 900,000 SAR with nothing to show for it.
  • Annual rent increases: Saudi rental contracts typically allow 5-10% annual increases. Over a decade, your rent can nearly double.
  • No customisation: Want to paint the walls, install better lighting, or landscape the garden? Most landlords say no, or you pay to restore the property when you leave.
  • Instability: The landlord may sell the property, forcing you to move with 90 days' notice. For families with children in school, this disruption can be traumatic.
  • Compound community fees: Many compounds charge separate annual fees for maintenance, pool, and security , adding 5,000-15,000 SAR/year to your costs.

Buying in Saudi Arabia: Costs and Security

Homeownership in Saudi has become significantly more accessible over the past decade, particularly for Saudi citizens. For expats, it remains a niche option but one that is growing in relevance.

On the surface, buying appears cheaper , and over a long enough timeline, it is. But the monthly mortgage figure does not include maintenance (1-2% of property value per year = 12,000-24,000 SAR), property insurance (~2,000 SAR/year), and the opportunity cost of that 322,000-450,000 SAR down payment that could have been invested elsewhere.

Advantages of Buying

  • Equity building: Every payment builds ownership. After 10 years, assuming 5% annual appreciation, a 1.2M SAR villa would be worth approximately 1.95M SAR, and you would have paid down about 200,000 SAR of principal.
  • Stability: No landlord, no 90-day notices, no annual renegotiation. Your children stay in the same school district. You can plant trees, build a pergola, and paint the walls turquoise if you want.
  • Legacy: For Saudi families especially, the home is generational wealth that passes to children. Real estate remains one of the most reliable asset classes in the Kingdom.
  • Sakani benefits (Saudi nationals only): Up to 500,000 SAR in interest-free lending from the Real Estate Development Fund, plus subsidised monthly payments for the first five years.
  • Hedge against inflation: Rent in Saudi Arabia has risen 6-8% annually in desirable neighbourhoods. Your mortgage payment is fixed (or adjusts slowly), making it cheaper in real terms over time.

Disadvantages of Buying

  • Massive upfront cost: 27-37% of a property's value in cash is out of reach for most families, especially expats who also face education fees and living expenses.
  • Illiquidity: Real estate takes months to sell. If you need to relocate quickly for a job, you may have to sell at a discount or rent out the property remotely.
  • Maintenance and repairs: Budget 1-2% of property value annually. A new AC unit costs 5,000-10,000 SAR. A roof repair can be 15,000 SAR+. These are now your expenses.
  • Job risk (especially for expats): If you lose your job and your iqama is cancelled, you typically have 30-90 days to leave the country , or to sell your property under pressure. This is the single biggest risk for expat homeowners.
  • Limited financing for expats: Max 70-80% LTV (Loan to Value), higher profit rates (5.5-7% vs 4-5.5% for Saudis), and shorter tenors (15-20 years vs 25-30 years).

Halal Mortgage (Murabaha) Options in Saudi Arabia

All mortgages in Saudi Arabia are structured as Islamic financing products, primarily Murabaha (cost-plus sale) and Ijara (lease-to-own). This is a critical distinction: there is no "interest" in the conventional sense, but rather a profit rate disclosed upfront.

Major Saudi banks offering residential mortgages include:

  • Al Rajhi Bank: The largest Islamic bank globally. Offers Murabaha financing up to 85% LTV for Saudis, 70% for expats. Profit rates from 4.2%.
  • National Commercial Bank (NCB): Now part of SNB. Offers competitive rates for both Saudi and expat customers with strong employment histories.
  • Riyad Bank: Known for flexible terms. Offers up to 25-year tenors for expats in stable employment.
  • Bank Albilad: Popular for its Sakani-aligned products and fast processing.
  • SAIB (Saudi Investment Bank): Offers competitive expat packages, particularly for professionals in healthcare and engineering.

For expats, typical requirements include: minimum salary of 15,000-20,000 SAR/month, minimum 2 years with current employer, valid iqama with at least 6 months remaining, and a clean credit history through SIMAH (Saudi Credit Bureau).

The Sakani Programme: What Saudi Families Need to Know

Sakani (formerly known as the Real Estate Development Fund programme) is the cornerstone of Saudi Arabia's push toward 70% homeownership. It offers:

  • Interest-free loans: Up to 500,000 SAR from the Real Estate Development Fund.
  • Subsidised monthly payments: In the first five years, the government covers a portion of the monthly payment.
  • Down payment support: Through the "Sakani Plus" programme, eligible families receive up to 150,000 SAR toward their down payment.
  • Ready-built homes: Through partnerships with real estate developers, Sakani offers fully constructed villas and apartments in designated suburbs at subsidised prices.

Eligibility: Saudi nationals aged 25+ who do not currently own a home. The application is through the Sakani platform (sakani.sa) and requires a valid national ID, proof of income, and a clean credit record.

Important: Sakani is for Saudi citizens only. Expat families do not qualify under any circumstances.

The break-even point occurs around year 6. Before that, renting is cheaper when accounting for upfront costs and maintenance. After year 7, buying clearly wins. If you plan to stay less than 5 years, rent. If more than 7, buy. In the 5-7 year zone, it is a close call depending on your risk tolerance and market conditions.

The School District Factor

For families with children, school catchment areas can be the deciding factor. In Riyadh, the best international schools cluster in specific districts , Al-Malqa, Al-Narjis, Al-Yarmouk, and the Diplomatic Quarter. Renting in these areas gives you the flexibility to move when your child changes schools or your second child starts at a different institution.

Buying locks you into a neighbourhood. If your child transfers to a school on the other side of the city, you face a long daily commute or the cost and hassle of selling and buying again. For families with multiple children at different school stages, renting near the best school cluster often makes more sense.

Final Verdict: Renting vs Buying

There is no universal right answer. The best choice depends on your citizenship, job stability, time horizon, savings, and family needs. But here are the bottom-line conclusions:

For Saudi families: If you are eligible for Sakani and plan to stay in the same city for 5+ years, buying is almost always the better choice. The government subsidies make mortgage payments cheaper than rent, and you build equity and generational wealth.

For expat families: Rent unless (a) you have a long-term contract with 5+ years remaining, (b) you have Premium Residency or are seriously pursuing it, and (c) you have the 30%+ down payment in cash. The risk of a forced sale due to job loss or iqama cancellation is simply too high for most expat families to justify the risk of ownership under a standard work visa.

The right decision is the one that gives your family security, aligns with your financial reality, and lets you sleep well at night. Whether that means a rented apartment in a vibrant compound or a owned villa with a garden you can call your own, Saudi Arabia's property market offers options for every family at every stage.

Is it cheaper to rent or buy in Riyadh?

In the short term (under 5 years), renting is cheaper. In the long term (over 7 years), buying typically wins due to equity building. For a villa worth 1.2M SAR, the break-even point is approximately year 6, assuming 5% annual rent increases.

Can expats get mortgages in Saudi Arabia?

Yes, expats can obtain Islamic mortgages (Murabaha) from Saudi banks. Requirements typically include a valid iqama, stable employment, a minimum salary (often 15,000 SAR/month), and a down payment of at least 20-30% of the property value. Financing is usually capped at 70-80% of the property price.

What is Sakani and how does it help first-time buyers?

Sakani is the Saudi government's housing programme offering subsidised mortgages and down payment support for first-time Saudi buyers. It provides interest-free loans of up to 500,000 SAR and reduces monthly payments through the Real Estate Development Fund. Sakani is for Saudi nationals only.

How much deposit do I need for a home in Saudi?

For Saudi nationals, the minimum down payment is typically 10-15% through Sakani programmes. For expats, banks require 20-30% down payment. On a 1M SAR property, that means 100,000-150,000 SAR for Saudis and 200,000-300,000 SAR for expats, plus 5% VAT and 2-3% in transaction fees.

Is buying a villa better than an apartment for families?

Villas generally offer more space, privacy, outdoor areas, and better resale value, making them ideal for families with children. Apartments are more affordable, easier to maintain, and located in central areas. The choice depends on budget, family size, and lifestyle preferences.
