The Power of I Was Wrong

Jun 21, 2026 10 min read

The Power of I Was Wrong

Apologize to your child this week. Not for something big for something small. For the tone you used. For the promise you forgot. For the moment you were distracted when they needed your attention. Many parents hesitate to apologize because they worry it will undermine their authority. The research says the opposite. A 2022 study from the University of Notre Dame found that children whose parents apologized had higher emotional intelligence, stronger trust in their parents, and healthier conflict resolution skills. An apology does not weaken authority. It strengthens relationship.

Why Parents Struggle to Apologize

Many parents grew up in homes where adults did not apologize. They learned that authority means never admitting fault. This model is deeply ingrained and difficult to break. But breaking it is one of the most important things a parent can do for their childrens emotional development. Children who see their parents apologize learn that accountability is a sign of strength not weakness. They internalize the understanding that everyone makes mistakes and that what matters is not the mistake but the repair. This lesson is foundational for their future marriages, friendships, and professional relationships. When you apologize to your child you are not just addressing a single incident. You are teaching them that relationships can be repaired. This is far more powerful than teaching the same lesson through words alone.

The Process of Repair

The process has three parts: acknowledgment, apology, and change. Acknowledgment means naming what you did wrong without minimizing or justifying. Say: I was wrong to raise my voice. I was wrong to dismiss your feelings. Apology means expressing regret and asking for forgiveness. Say: I am sorry. Will you forgive me? Change means making a concrete commitment to do better. Say: Next time I will take a deep breath before responding. Children are remarkably sensitive to all three parts. They can tell when an apology is genuine and when it is performed. A genuine apology given once is worth more than a hundred performed apologies given daily.

The Longer Term Impact

Parents who make apology a regular practice report that their children become more honest about their own mistakes. When children see that adults can admit fault, they learn that mistakes are not shameful. This reduces the likelihood of lying and hiding errors. Over time, the family develops a culture of honesty, vulnerability, and growth. This culture is the foundation of true peace in the home. Traditional Islamic teachings on tawbah repentance emphasize the same principles: acknowledge the mistake, feel genuine regret, commit to change, and seek forgiveness. When parents model this process, they give their children a spiritual and emotional framework that will serve them for life.

This Week's Practice

Find one moment today to apologize to your child for something small. Look them in the eye. Say: I was wrong. I am sorry. Do not make excuses or explain why you did it. Let the apology stand alone. Notice how they respond. Often children forgive instantly. Sometimes they need time. Respect their process. Repeat this practice daily for one week and observe the shift in your familys emotional climate. The apology is not about getting forgiveness. It is about taking responsibility. One apology at a time, peace starts at home.

The Courage to Be Imperfect

Perfectionism is one of the greatest barriers to authentic parenting. Many parents believe they must have all the answers and never make mistakes. This belief creates pressure that strains relationships and reduces the joy of parenting. When a parent apologizes, they model a different way: the way of humility, growth, and repair. This is far more valuable than the appearance of perfection. Children do not need perfect parents. They need parents who are honest, present, and willing to grow.

The psychological safety that comes from knowing that mistakes can be repaired creates an environment where children feel safe to take risks, make their own mistakes, and learn from them. This safety is one of the most important gifts a parent can give. It requires nothing more than the willingness to say three words: I was wrong. These three words, spoken genuinely and regularly, can transform a family. They signal that the home is a place where everyone is learning, including the parents. This is the foundation of a growth mindset in children, which research has shown to be one of the strongest predictors of success and wellbeing.

How to Make Apology a Habit

Start with one apology per day for one week. It does not have to be for something major. Apologize for being distracted, for rushing, for raising your voice, for forgetting a promise. The specificity of the apology matters more than the size of the mistake. After one week, notice any shifts in your childs behavior. Are they more willing to admit their own mistakes? Do they seem more relaxed? Do they initiate conversations more often? These small shifts are evidence that the practice is working. Continue for a second week, and then a third. By the end of the month, apology will feel natural rather than forced. It will have become part of your familys culture, and the peace that follows will be its own reward.

The Ripple Effect of an Apology

When a parent apologizes to a child, the effects extend beyond that single moment. Children who experience genuine apologies from their parents develop a stronger internal sense of right and wrong. They learn that relationships require maintenance and repair. They understand that love is not about perfection but about showing up, making mistakes, and trying again. These lessons shape how they interact with siblings, friends, and eventually their own partners and children. The apology you give today may influence how your child parents their own children decades from now. This is the ripple effect of accountability, and it is one of the most powerful forces for healing in family life.

The Islamic tradition places great emphasis on the concept of tawbah, which includes acknowledgment, remorse, restitution, and commitment to change. When parents model tawbah in their relationships with their children, they give their children a living example of Islamic ethics. The child learns that repentance is not just a ritual between a person and Allah but also a practical tool for repairing human relationships. This integration of spiritual practice and daily life is at the heart of Islamic parenting. One apology, sincerely given, can be a moment of spiritual growth for both parent and child.

Every family has the power to choose a different path. The first step is simply questioning the default: Do we need this? Does this serve our family? Is there a simpler way? These questions, asked consistently over time, lead to a life that is not less but more: more time, more presence, more peace. The engineer who simplified did not lose anything he truly needed. He gained what he truly wanted: a family that lives with intention rather than on autopilot. His story is an invitation to every family to consider what they might gain by letting go.

The apology you give today is a seed planted in your childs heart. It will grow into a forest of trust, honesty, and emotional safety that will shelter your family for years to come. Do not underestimate the power of three words spoken with sincerity: I was wrong. I am sorry. These words, repeated consistently over time, build a family culture where everyone feels safe to be imperfect, and where love is not conditional on performance.

Parenting is not about being right. It is about being in relationship. And relationships require repair. Each time you apologize, you demonstrate that your childs feelings matter more than your need to be right. This is the essence of respectful parenting, and it is accessible to every parent regardless of education, income, or background. All it takes is the willingness to say three words: I was wrong.

A Living-Room Test

Imagine a Thursday evening in Riyadh. After a sharp reply at dinner, a father waited until the children finished homework, sat at eye level, and said: I was wrong to answer you like that. I am sorry. No lecture followed. The room softened faster than any motivational speech. The vignette is illustrative, but the pattern is reliable: repair works when ownership is specific and excuses are scarce.

Try this sequence once this week with a spouse or child: name the act, name the impact, ask forgiveness, state one behaviour you will change, then listen without defending. If the other person needs time, give it. An apology is an opening, not a receipt for instant absolution.

In homes where pride is prized, the words I was wrong can feel like loss of status. Reframe them as leadership. The person who restores connection protects the family climate more than the person who wins the argument. Over a month of small repairs, trust compounds the way resentment used to.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a real apology and a formal one?
A formal apology stops at the word sorry without change. A real apology combines owning the mistake, empathy for the other person, and a concrete commitment not to repeat it, followed by visible behaviour change.
What if I apologize and my spouse does not accept it?
Keep a sincere apology without nagging for an instant reply. Acceptance can take time, especially if the same hurt happened before. What matters most is that your spouse sees real change in daily behaviour.
How do I teach children to apologize in practice?
Start with yourself. Apologize to your child sincerely when you are wrong. Children copy what they see more than they absorb lectures. Model accountability as strength, not weakness.
Does repeating the same apology for the same mistake still matter?
Not really. Apology without behaviour change loses credibility and becomes an empty word the other person stops trusting.
Who should apologize first in a marital conflict?
The person who starts is usually the emotionally mature one, regardless of who was more wrong in percentage terms. Starting breaks the silence loop and opens dialogue before the conflict grows.
