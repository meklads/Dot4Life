Family Summer Volunteering

Jun 23, 2026 10 min read

A Gulf Family Volunteered Together This Summer - And Their Children Changed

According to WHO, "Can we go again next week?" her nine-year-old son asked, holding a bag of food for their elderly neighbor. The mother, used to hearing "I'm bored" and "When are we going home?" on every family outing, couldn't believe this question came from her own child. That was the sentence she never expected - and it changed how she saw giving, family, and summer break altogether.

The Challenge: A Summer Between Screens and Emptiness

Like many Gulf families, Umm Khaled faced the summer dilemma: her children spent hours glued to tablets and video games. She felt guilty watching the days slip by without real benefit, yet she was exhausted from suggesting "fun" activities that turned into complaints within half an hour.

"I sat with my husband at the start of summer and said: this year we need something different. Not just outings and events. Something that makes the kids feel truly important."

That's how it began. Not with a ten-point master plan, but with a simple decision: two hours every week, the whole family together, doing volunteer work suitable for their children's ages.

Two Hours a Week Changed Everything

They started with a small step: preparing food baskets for families in need in their neighborhood. The task was straightforward: buy basic staples (rice, oil, sugar, dates, milk), have the children pack and arrange the baskets, then distribute them together through the local charity.

Khaled (age 9) says: "I put the dates and rice in the bag, and Dad helps me tie it. One time we gave a basket to Uncle Abdullah - he's not really my uncle, but everyone in the neighborhood calls him that - and he was so happy to see us." Mohammed (13), who had refused any family activity claiming he was "too old," volunteered to organize books at the local library every Thursday morning. After two weeks, he became the one reminding his parents about library day.

Five Steps for Any Family Wanting to Start Volunteering

Based on this family's experience and others who tried the same path:

1. Start Very Small (One to Two Hours Weekly)

Don't plan massive projects from week one. A small, consistent commitment builds a habit, while an ambitious plan fizzles out after the first attempt. One fixed weekly hour is enough to build a sense of responsibility.

2. Choose Age-Appropriate Activities

Food basket packing suits children aged 5 to 12. Library organizing fits teenagers. Visiting the elderly (through organized charities, not individually) works for children 10 and above with parents. The key is that the child feels they're genuinely contributing - not just tagging along.

3. Make It a Fixed Weekly Appointment

A fixed time eliminates the "now or later" negotiations and turns volunteering into routine. "We chose Thursday morning. The kids now know Thursday is giving day. They actually make sure not to miss it."

4. Let Children Choose the Activity

When a child feels they chose, they commit more. Offer two or three options - all good volunteering choices - and let them decide. A child who picked "help at the library" because it's near grandmother's house will go eagerly because the decision was theirs.

5. Celebrate the Impact, Not the Photo

Instead of saying "you look great in this picture with the basket," say "I noticed how happy Abu Mohammed was with the food. You really made his day." Praise tied to impact builds internal motivation for giving; praise tied to image builds attachment to social appearance.

Types of Family Volunteering in the Gulf

Not all volunteering opportunities suit children. Here are the most family-appropriate options available across Gulf countries:

  • Food basket packing and distribution: Available at most charities - Al-Bir Society in Saudi Arabia and Emirates Charity Association in the UAE. Suitable for children aged 5 and up.
  • Public libraries: Organizing books, helping with events. Suitable for teenagers. King Abdulaziz Public Library and Qatar National Library both have family volunteering programs.
  • Environmental activities: Beach cleanups (like Saudi's "Saheli" initiative), tree planting in public parks. Suitable for children aged 7 and up.
  • Visiting elderly care homes: Only through organized programs via official institutions, not individual visits. Mohammed bin Rashid Foundation for Elderly Care is one example.
  • Summer center assistance: Some centers allow families to volunteer organizing activities for younger children.

The key is choosing an activity where your children can see the impact with their own eyes. Visible impact - the face of an elderly person who smiled, a cleaner park - is what connects a child to the meaning of giving.

What Actually Changed in the Children?

After a month, the parents noticed changes they hadn't expected:

  • Conversation at the dinner table: Instead of complaints and requests, the children started telling stories about people they met during volunteering. Family lunch became a space for stories, not demands.
  • Sense of worth: Mohammed the teenager, who felt he "wasn't good at anything," now took pride in being responsible for the Islamic books section at the library. When his mother asked why he was so enthusiastic, he said: "People are actually reading the books I organized."
  • Patience and calm: "I noticed Khaled became more patient with his little sister. Maybe because he saw that giving requires patience."
  • Asking to give, not to get: Instead of "I want a toy," the question became "Can we help someone today?" - a shift in the daily language of need.

These changes don't happen overnight. They're the result of repetition and exposure, not a single lesson. Week one was chaotic. Week two saw the first signs of enthusiasm. Month one built the habit. Parents need as much patience as children.

Why Family Volunteering Works When Lectures Fail

Children don't learn values from words - they learn from experiences. When a child participates in distributing food to a family in need, they don't just hear the word "giving" - they live it. This direct experience has been shown in educational research (Early Childhood Research Quarterly) to affect children's behavior more deeply than any verbal instruction. The difference between saying and doing: words stay in the ear, actions stay in memory and muscle.

Not Every Week Was Perfect

Let's be honest: some weeks they failed. A week when the children were sick, a travel week, a "we just didn't have the energy" week. What mattered was that they came back the following week. Family volunteering isn't a test of perfection - it's training in consistency. The mother says: "This experience taught me something important: our children learn more from our return after failure than from our uninterrupted success."

Start Tomorrow

You don't need official registration or a major charity organization. You can start tomorrow: ask yourselves - what skill does our family have that could help others? Some families arrange weekly visits with elderly neighbors, others join the "Goodness Basket" initiatives found in most Gulf neighborhoods, and others volunteer at public libraries or summer centers.

What matters is starting together. Not because volunteering is "good for the kids" - but because it builds a shared family memory: "Remember when we distributed dates to the neighbors?" That memory is worth more than any expensive summer camp.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: My children are young (3-5 years). Can they participate?

A: Yes, in simple ways. A young child can choose an old toy to donate, or draw a greeting card to place with a food basket. The important thing is that they feel part of the family action, not just a spectator.

Q: How do we find suitable volunteering opportunities in our city?

A: Look at local charity websites or platforms like Nawen (nawen.sa) in Saudi Arabia, or volunteer programs at public libraries. Most have sections for family volunteering.

Q: My teenagers are not enthusiastic. What should I do?

A: Don't force them. Start with a very short activity (half an hour) and include their friends if possible. Teens respond to group experiences with peers more than to family values alone.

Q: We're concerned about our children's safety in volunteer settings.

A: This is why we recommend in-kind donations (preparing food baskets) rather than direct distribution for young children. Library and environmental activities are safer. Choose a trusted organization with a known reputation in your community.

Q: What if we miss a week?

A: That's perfectly normal. Some weeks will fail - illness, travel, exhaustion. What matters is returning the following week. Family volunteering is not a test of perfection but training in consistency. **CTA:** "Plan your weekly giving hour together: use the daily planner to set your family volunteering time and track your weekly commitments."
