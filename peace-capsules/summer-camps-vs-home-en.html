Summer Camps vs Home: Kids Guide

Jun 23, 2026 10 min read

According to public health guidance, 450 SAR per week for a summer camp, versus a home program at half the cost with more family time. Which option suits your child this summer? The answer isn't the same for every family - it depends on your child's age, your budget, the time you have available, and what you want this summer to be.

This guide helps you weigh both options with clear criteria, not just impressions.

Six Questions to Ask Before You Decide

1. What's the actual cost?

In Riyadh, organized camps range from 300 to 700 SAR per week, according to Time Out Riyadh's 2026 summer camp directory. Home activities cost less but require more parental time and planning.

2. How much time do you have?

If both parents work full-time, an organized camp is a practical solution. If one parent is available or works flexibly, home activities are feasible.

3. What's your child's age?

Children under 6 benefit more from a calm home routine with parents. Ages 7-12 gain from social interaction at camps. Teenagers 13+ may prefer specialized programs (volunteering, skill courses).

4. What's your summer goal?

Specific skill development (swimming, coding) → specialized camp. Building family memories and releasing energy → home program.

5. What's your child's temperament?

A social child who loves group play → camp. A child who prefers quiet and solo activities → home program. Sometimes mixing both is the best solution.

6. What about distance and logistics?

A camp one hour away means two hours daily in transit. Is the benefit worth the time cost?

When Does Camp Make Sense?

Camp is an excellent choice when:

  • Both parents work and can't provide daily supervision
  • Your child needs intensive social interaction with peers
  • You want professional instruction in a specific skill (swimming, coding)
  • Your child gets bored quickly and needs a varied daily program

Umm Sara from Jeddah says: "My son spent all day on his iPad and rejected every suggestion from me. We tried a swimming camp. From the first week, he had friends and outings waiting for him every day. Camp didn't solve everything, but it broke the boredom and gave him something to look forward to."

When Does a Home Program Work Better?

A home program is better when:

  • Your budget is limited and camp costs are a burden
  • You have time for supervision and participation (part-time work, vacation, stay-at-home parent)
  • Your family prefers a custom routine matching your values and interests
  • Your child is shy, prefers quiet environments, or doesn't enjoy large groups
  • You want a family-centered summer, not just a busy daily schedule

Umm Khaled from Riyadh: "Four children - camp costs ×4 is impossible. We set up an activity corner at home: a weekly schedule, art day, baking with me day, short trip day. The kids enjoyed it more than I expected."

The Hidden Cost of Camp: More Than the Registration Fee

When calculating camp costs, don't look only at the registration fee. There are additional expenses:

  • Transportation: Daily drop-off and pick-up = fuel + time.
  • Meals: Some camps don't include meals or sell them above market price.
  • Equipment: Sports gear, swimwear, hats, sunscreen - these add up quickly.
  • Extra activities: Field trips may come with additional fees.

Home programs have hidden costs too: parental time (your most valuable resource), activity materials, and the mental energy of daily planning.

The Middle Ground: Mixing Both

You don't have to choose one over the other. Many families blend options:

  • Two weeks at a specialized camp (sports or swimming) to burn energy
  • The rest of summer with a flexible home program on a smaller budget
  • Or: two camp days per week, the rest at home

This mix gives children the best of both worlds: social interaction and structured learning + family time and creative freedom.

Practical Steps to Design Your Children's Summer

  1. Sit together as parents and set your summer budget and goals. How much can you allocate for summer activities? What skills do you want your children to develop? Set a realistic ceiling and stick to it.
  1. Discuss options with your children (age-appropriately). Ask them: What do you want to do this summer? Write all ideas down, then filter by feasibility. Surprise: sometimes children choose simple home activities over fancy camps because they want time with you, not a packed schedule.
  1. Book camps early. Good camps fill up weeks before summer starts. Early booking may also get you a discount of up to 20% at some centers.
  1. Design a weekly home routine for the remaining time. Even if you choose camp, free days and gap weeks need a plan. Don't leave them to empty boredom - that's summer's biggest enemy.
  1. Evaluate after a month. Try, then decide: continue or change? Some children need to try camp to discover they don't like it - and vice versa. Monthly review prevents sticking to a choice that doesn't serve your child.

How to Design an Excellent Home Program on a Limited Budget

If you choose the home route, these ideas help you maximize value at minimum cost:

  • A varied weekly routine: Art day (drawing, crafts), baking and cooking with mom, reading and library visit day, outdoor activity day (park, beach, short trip).
  • Use public libraries: Most Gulf public libraries offer free or nearly free children's summer activities.
  • Neighborhood activity swaps: One day at your house, one day at the neighbor's - children play together while parents rotate supervision.
  • Life skills: Summer is the perfect time to teach practical skills: making the bed, doing laundry, preparing a simple meal. These are things camps don't teach.

Practical lock-in after reading

Turn the idea into one action this week and place it somewhere visible. A short seven-day review beats enthusiasm on reading day alone.

If resistance appears, shrink the size rather than canceling the habit. Calm continuity builds family trust in any new change.

Share the plan with a partner or friend who asks one weekly question: did you do the step? Gentle accountability protects quality.

Q: Are summer camps safe for my children?

A: Most licensed camps in the Gulf are regulated by official authorities. Look for camps licensed by the Ministry of Human Resources or the municipality. Ask directly about the supervisor-to-child ratio (ideal: 1 supervisor per 8-10 children). Visit the camp before registering if possible, and confirm insurance coverage.

Q: My son refuses camp on day one. Should I force him?

A: No. Ask why specifically: shy around new children? Didn't like the activity? Instructor too strict? Some children need a week to adjust. If refusal continues after 3 days with no signs of improvement, look for alternatives. Summer isn't boot camp - it's time for rest and enjoyment.

Q: What's the best age for summer camp?

A: 7-12 years is the golden age for camps. Children at this stage are social enough and can manage without parents for hours. Under 6, a home program is usually better.

Q: How do I balance camp with my budget?

A: Don't stretch your budget for a camp. Two weeks at a specialized camp is enough to deliver benefits, and the rest of summer can be an excellent home program on a modest budget. Remember: 600 SAR for two weeks of camp might mean 3,000 SAR for the whole summer - is that in your budget?

Practical Tips for Summer Camps vs Home Activities

Summer camps and a structured home summer both work, the deciding factor is usually less about cost and more about what a specific child needs that year: a shy child might benefit from a camp's social push, while a child who had an exhausting school year might do better with unstructured time at home.

If choosing a camp, book early, popular Gulf summer camps fill up by April, and confirm the adult-to-child ratio and what happens during the hottest midday hours before paying a deposit, since not all camps handle Gulf summer heat with equal care.

If keeping kids home, resist the urge to fill every hour. A loose daily rhythm, a fixed wake time, one planned activity, free play, and a consistent bedtime, gives structure without turning the summer into a second school term.

Conclusion

Whichever route a family chooses, the summer that sticks in a child's memory is usually shaped by a handful of genuinely fun days, not by whether those days happened at a camp or at home. Plan for a few standout outings either way, and let the rest of the summer be ordinary.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best first step?

Start with one small habit for two weeks before adding more.

How much time do I need daily?

Ten to twenty steady minutes beat a rare long session.

What if I miss a week?

Return calmly without self-attack and restart the smallest step.

Must the whole family join?

Ideally, but begin with whoever is ready and expand gradually.

When should I seek a professional?

When harm continues or you cannot adjust after sincere attempts.

What can I do tonight?

Pick one action you can finish in fifteen minutes.
