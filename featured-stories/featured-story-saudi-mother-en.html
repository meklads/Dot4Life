A Saudi Mother's Journey: Balancing Work and Parenting

Apr 10, 2026 8 min read

Introduction: Noura's Story

At 6:00 AM, Noura, a Saudi mother of three children aged 4 to 10, wakes up to start a long day. She has to prepare breakfast, drop the children at school, arrive at her consulting firm job before 8:00, pick up the children in the afternoon, help with homework, cook dinner, and before bed find an inbox full of pending work tasks. Noura is not alone in her daily struggle. She is one of more than one and a half million working Saudi women who face the same difficult equation every day: how to be an excellent mother and a successful employee at the same time.

Two years ago, Noura was about to resign. The pressure was immense, and guilt followed her both at work and at home. But instead of giving up, she began a journey of research, trial, and error to find a system that worked for her. Today, she is not only able to balance her responsibilities but has become an informal coach for her younger mother colleagues at the company. In this article, Noura shares her experience honestly: the challenges, the failures, and the solutions that changed her life.

Noura's story reflects a reality millions live. According to the International Labour Organization, working women perform twice as much household work as men on average (ILO Report). This disparity is one of the main reasons for the immense pressure working mothers face.

Why Working Mothers Face Double Pressure

Before exploring Noura's solutions, it is important to understand the roots of the challenge. A working mother faces not just time management pressure but complex psychological and social pressures:

Sources of Pressure on Working Mothers
SourceDescriptionImpact
Work demandsLong hours, meetings, piled-up tasks, performance pressurePhysical and mental exhaustion
Family demandsChildcare, school follow-up, household tasks, family visitsConstant feeling of not doing enough
Social pressureSociety's view of working mothers, comparisons, unrealistic expectationsGuilt and internal conflict
Internal pressurePerfectionism, desire to prove oneself, fear of failureChronic anxiety and psychological fatigue

Noura recalls: "I felt I was failing at everything. At work, I was the least productive employee because my mind was with my children. At home, I was a tired mother who could not play with her kids. It took me time to understand that the problem was not me, but my life system that needed redesigning."

Challenges Noura Faced

Honestly, Noura lists the challenges that almost pushed her to resign:

  1. Chronic time shortage: Juggling long work hours (8 AM to 5 PM) with household duties and caring for three children was nearly impossible. Her day started at 5:30 AM and ended after midnight without real rest.
  2. Psychological pressure and guilt: At work she felt guilty for leaving her children. With her children she felt guilty for not completing work tasks. This internal conflict is psychologically exhausting.
  3. Lack of support: Her husband helped but in a limited way due to his own work, and extended family lived far away. There was no real "team" supporting her.
  4. Financial challenge: The nanny's salary consumed a large portion of her income, making her question whether working was economically viable after childcare and transportation costs.
  5. No personal time: Even on weekends, household tasks filled her time. The last time she read a book was six months ago, and the last time she went out with friends was a year ago.
Honest Moment

Noura says: "One night, I was preparing dinner and crying silently. My little son asked me: 'Mommy, why are you always tired?' In that moment, I realized I needed a radical change, not just for me, but for my children too."

Solutions That Changed Noura's Life

After months of research and experimentation, Noura developed a set of practical solutions that completely changed her life. She shares them hoping to help other mothers:

1. Weekly, Not Daily, Planning

Instead of daily planning that exhausted her, Noura switched to weekly planning. Every Sunday, she sits for an hour and plans her entire week: work appointments, children's activities, household tasks, and rest time. This broader planning gives her more flexibility and reduces daily anxiety.

2. The 8-8-8 Rule

Noura now divides her day into three main time blocks: 8 hours for sleep, 8 hours for work (including commute), and 8 hours for family and rest. This simple rule helped her set clear boundaries and prevent work from creeping into family time and vice versa.

3. Fair Task Distribution

Noura sat with her husband and they clearly divided household tasks. He handles morning school drop-offs and homework help, while she manages evenings and home organization. The important thing was that the distribution was not perfect but fair and agreed upon. Noura says: "The problem is not the amount of work, but the feeling that you are solely responsible for everything."

4. Quality Over Perfection

This was the hardest change for Noura. She learned to accept that the home will not always be perfect, dinner may sometimes be simple, and children may watch more TV than ideal. What truly matters is the quality of time with children: full focus, away from phone and work thoughts. One hour of good play with a child is better than three distracted hours.

5. Building a Support Network

Instead of accepting the lack of support, Noura systematically built a support network: carpool rotation with other school mothers (each mother drives once a week), hiring a part-time nanny, and joining a working mothers group on WhatsApp for advice and emotional support.

6. Government Support Programs

In Saudi Arabia, there are growing government initiatives to support working mothers. The Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development (official ministry website) offers programs regulating women's work and protecting their rights, including paid maternity leave and prohibiting job discrimination. Saudi Vision 2030 has opened wide fields for women's work and provided a more supportive environment than ever before.

7. Self-Care Without Guilt

Noura now dedicates two hours weekly for herself: one for reading at a quiet cafe, one for exercise. She says: "I realized that taking time for rest is not selfish. It is necessary. I deserve this time because I work hard." Studies confirm that mothers who allocate time for themselves are more patient and productive at work (WHO Mental Health at Work Report).

Results of Change

After a year of applying these solutions, Noura's life changed noticeably. She is calmer and more productive, and her relationship with her children has deepened. She says: "My children no longer see a constantly tired mother. They see a woman who loves her work, loves her family, and loves herself too." Key results include:

  • Her work performance improved and she received a promotion after being on the verge of resigning.
  • Her relationship with her husband improved after family partnership became clearer.
  • Her children became more independent as she gradually taught them to take responsibility.
  • Her stress levels dropped, and her mental and physical health improved.

Tips for Every Working Mother

Based on Noura's experience and expert advice, here are practical steps you can start today:

  1. Stop perfectionism: There is no perfect mother or perfect employee. Start by being satisfied that you are doing your best with the circumstances available to you.
  2. Ask for help boldly: Talking about your needs is not weakness. Tell your husband, family, and friends clearly: "I need help with this."
  3. Use technology wisely: Shared calendar apps, task lists, voice reminders - all save precious time.
  4. Negotiate with your employer: In Saudi Arabia, there is a growing trend toward flexible and remote work. Propose flexible options to your manager.
  5. Do not compare yourself to others: Every mother has her own circumstances and abilities. What works for others may not work for you. Create your own path with conviction.

Conclusion: A Message of Hope

Noura's story is not a fairy tale of success but a real struggle of an ordinary mother who chose to face challenges rather than surrender. The lesson is not in reaching perfect balance but in continuing to try and learn from mistakes. Every day is a new opportunity for better balance.

Remember: you are not alone. Thousands of working mothers in Saudi Arabia and the Arab world face the same challenges. Sharing experiences and mutual support make the journey lighter. As Noura says: "A happy working mother is not one who does everything perfectly, but one who knows when to work hard and when to allow herself to rest."

Disclaimer: The information in this article is based on personal experiences and public sources, for guidance only. Every family situation is unique, and professional consultation is recommended when needed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I balance work and childcare?
Advance planning is key. Create a weekly schedule distributing your time between work and family. Use time management apps, seek support from your partner and family. Remember that balance is not perfect every day, but a continuous effort.
What are the best time management tips for working mothers?
Divide your day into blocks: work, children, home, and yourself. The 8-8-8 rule helps: 8 hours sleep, 8 hours work, 8 hours family and rest. The most important thing is setting clear boundaries between work time and family time.
How can I overcome working mother guilt?
Remember that working is not neglecting your children but investing in their future. You are modeling hard work and independence. Quality time with your children matters more than quantity. Focus on meaningful moments.
How do I choose a suitable nursery or nanny?
Look for licensed nurseries recommended by other mothers. Make surprise visits, ask about caregiver qualifications and child-to-staff ratios. Trust your intuition after meeting the caregivers. Personal referrals from trusted friends are invaluable.
How can I ask my husband for more support?
Open communication is key. Discuss your needs with appreciation rather than blame. Divide tasks based on each person's strengths and schedule. Remember that marriage is a partnership and parenting is a shared responsibility.
