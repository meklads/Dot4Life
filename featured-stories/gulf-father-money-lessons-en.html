How a Gulf Father Taught His Kids the Value of Money Before Age 10

Jun 08, 2026 8 min read

In a quiet neighborhood in Riyadh, Sameer, a father of three (ages 8, 6, and 4), kept facing the same frustrating scene: his children asking for every toy they saw at the store, crying if he didn't buy it, and forgetting about it the next day. Sameer didn't want to raise materialistic kids, but he also didn't want them to believe money grows on trees.

The challenge: How do I teach my children the value of money before TV commercials teach them that everything must be bought instantly?

Two years ago, Sameer decided to try something different. Instead of weekly lectures about "money doesn't come easy," he designed a practical system suited to his children's ages. What happened next changed his family's relationship with money completely.

The Turning Point

It started on a weekend. Sameer gathered his children and said: "Each of you will get your monthly allowance upfront, and we will follow a small plan together." The amount was modest, but the way they handled it made all the difference. Instead of spending it all in the first week, Sameer helped them divide it into three parts: free spending, saving for something big they wanted, and sharing (charity).

What surprised Sameer was that his oldest son (8) started asking deep questions: "If I save for two months, can I buy the bicycle I want?" "Can I lend my little brother from my allowance if he needs it?" Sameer realized that children understand more than we think. They need tools, not lectures.

Five Practical Lessons from Sameer's Experience

Lesson 1: The Three Jars System

  • Savings Jar: For something big later. Teaches delayed gratification.
  • Spending Jar: Free choice to buy what they want now, even if they make mistakes.
  • Giving Jar: A gift for a sibling, friend, or someone in need. Builds empathy.

Each child writes their name and goal on each jar. One month is enough for the child to see results with their own eyes.

Lesson 2: Weekly, Not Daily Allowance

A weekly allowance gives the child a chance to plan: "If I spend everything today, nothing will be left for the rest of the week." A child who makes mistakes for a week or two learns the lesson without a lecture. Sameer started with a small amount (20 SAR weekly for his oldest) and gave him full freedom to spend, with the right to discuss the outcome only after the mistake.

Lesson 3: A Mini Market at Home

Every Thursday, Sameer opens the "mini market": he buys small items (snacks, small toys, stationery) and sells them to his children at cost. The money they pay comes from their savings. This exercise mirrors the real market: if you spend on chocolate today, you will not be able to buy the toy you want next week. The lesson is tough but safe.

Lesson 4: Leading by Example

Sameer didn't stop at theory. Once a month, he sat with his children and showed them his simple notebook (paper, not an app): how much he saved that month, what he spent, and whether he achieved his small goal. Children imitate what they see more than what they hear. Watching their father plan his budget taught them more than any direct lesson.

Lesson 5: The Small Business Project

After six months, Sameer suggested a small project: a lemonade stand during the summer break or selling homemade treats to neighbors. Each child participated according to their age. The earnings were divided between spending, saving, and giving. For the first time, the children understood the principle of earning: money comes from effort, not from an ATM.

The Results After One Year

After a year, Sameer noticed clear changes: his children started asking for prices before requesting purchases. The oldest began saving for a long-term goal (a tablet) and took 4 months to achieve it. The youngest (4) started dividing his coins among the three jars on his own with understanding. Most importantly, family conversations about money increased, and crying fits at the supermarket decreased.

The Bottom Line: Start Now, Not When They Are Older

Children learn the value of money through practice, not words. Sameer's system was not complicated. All he did was turn money from a forbidden topic into a teaching tool. The only real mistake is waiting until the teenage years. Start today, even with a small allowance and three jars.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

At what age should I start teaching my child about money?
You can start as early as age 4-5 with simple concepts like waiting to buy something. Ages 6-7 are perfect for a weekly allowance. By ages 8-10, children can manage multiple savings jars (spending, saving, giving). Each stage builds on the previous one.
How do I explain the difference between saving and spending?
Use two or three clear jars labeled: Savings, Spending, Giving. Every time your child receives money, have them divide it among the jars themselves. This visual lesson teaches more than any lecture. The child watches their savings grow with their own eyes.
Should allowance be tied to chores?
A mixed approach works best: a fixed weekly amount teaches responsibility, while extra pay for optional tasks encourages initiative. Basic chores like making the bed and cleaning the room should not be paid because they are personal responsibilities. Extra tasks like washing the car can have compensation.
How much pocket money is appropriate for a child in the Gulf?
The amount varies by age and family income, but the principle is consistency. Start with a small regular amount given weekly so the child learns to plan. Around 10-20 SAR per week for ages 6-8 is a common starting range, increasing gradually with age and responsibility.
How do I balance teaching saving without depriving my child?
The goal is not deprivation but empowerment. Teach your child that money is a tool for achieving bigger goals. A simple rule: one-third for free spending, two-thirds for saving toward a larger goal. This builds balance without deprivation. And remember: leading by example matters more than instruction.
