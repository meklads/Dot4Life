The Expat Who Built a New Life in Saudi Arabia , A Story of Faith, Work, and Belonging

According to WHO, When Ahmed and Fatima packed their lives into eight suitcases and boarded a flight from Cairo to Riyadh in 2019, they had no idea the Kingdom would become more of a home than the one they left behind. What began as a three-year career contract for Ahmed , a senior mechanical engineer offered a role with Saudi Aramco , turned into a permanent relocation, a thriving home-based business, two children who now speak Arabic with a Saudi dialect, and a deep sense of belonging that neither of them expected.

This is the story of one family's journey. But it is also a guide for every expat family considering a move to the Gulf's largest and most transformative nation. Under Vision 2030, Saudi Arabia has opened its doors wider than ever before. The question is no longer "Should I move here?" but "How do I make it work when I do?"

Why They Moved: The Push and the Pull

The decision was not impulsive. Ahmed had spent twelve years in the Egyptian oil and gas sector, earning a respectable salary but feeling the ceiling of his career growth. When the Aramco offer landed , a 40% pay increase, housing allowance, children's education benefits, and annual home tickets , he calculated for two weeks before saying yes.

"In Egypt, I was comfortable but stuck," Ahmed says. "In Saudi, I saw a future for my children that I could not give them back home. That was the real calculation."

Fatima was harder to convince. A pharmacist by training, she worried about leaving her career, her mother, and the social fabric she had built over thirty years. "I cried the first week," she admits. "The apartment felt like a hotel, not a home. Everything was unfamiliar , the streets, the dialect, even the way people shopped for vegetables."

Yet both agree on one thing: the career opportunity was the door, but what made them stay was something deeper. Saudi Arabia offered not just a job, but a whole ecosystem for family life , safe streets, strong community values rooted in Islam, excellent schools, and a growing economy that made them feel part of something larger.

The First Year: Heat, Bureaucracy, and Homesickness

The first summer hit like a physical force. "I had read about Gulf heat," Fatima recalls. "But reading and living in 48°C are two different things. The car door handle burned my hand. You could not walk to the corner shop , you drove everywhere. I felt trapped indoors for three months."

The bureaucracy was the second shock. Obtaining the family iqama (residence permit) required multiple trips to the passport office, document attestations from the Egyptian Ministry of Foreign Affairs, a medical examination that took an entire morning, and a final fingerprinting session that tested every ounce of patience. "In the first month, we spent more time in government offices than in our own home," Ahmed says wryly.

But the hardest part was the quiet loneliness. The children, then aged 6 and 9, struggled at school. "My daughter came home crying because she said the Saudi kids laughed at her Egyptian accent," Fatima remembers. "For a mother, that is the deepest kind of pain , watching your child struggle and wondering if you made the wrong decision."

The first six months are the hardest for any expat family moving to Saudi Arabia. The heat disorients, the paperwork frustrates, and the absence of familiar faces creates a hollow ache that no video call can fill. But then something shifts.

Finding Community: The Mosque and the School

For Ahmed and Fatima, the turning point came through two institutions that form the heart of Saudi social life: the neighbourhood mosque and the international school.

Ahmed began attending Isha prayer at the local mosque, a modest building a five-minute walk from their compound. There he met men from Pakistan, Sudan, India, Syria, and Jordan , a cross-section of the global Muslim ummah living in Riyadh. "One brother invited me for tea after Maghrib. Another offered to help with my iqama renewal. A third introduced me to his cousin who owned a car dealership," Ahmed recounts. "The mosque is not just a place of worship here. It is the social hub of the expat community."

For Fatima, the real breakthrough was the school community. The international school their children attended , a British-curriculum school in the Al-Malqa district , had a vibrant parents' group. A WhatsApp chat for mothers in her daughter's class became her lifeline. She began attending coffee mornings, joined a weekly book club, and slowly, the loneliness receded.

"I realised that every expat mother was going through the same thing," she says. "The Sri Lankan mother, the British mother, the Sudanese mother , we all missed home, we all worried about our children, we all struggled with the heat. Sharing that struggle made it bearable."

When the Wife Started a Home Business

One of the most inspiring chapters of this family's story is what Fatima built. Unable to work as a pharmacist without Saudi licence equivalency (a process that can take up to two years), she turned her frustration into enterprise.

"I could not sit at home doing nothing for two years," she says firmly. "I had always baked at home in Cairo , kunafa, basbousa, maamoul. My friends began asking if they could buy from me. One order became ten, ten became fifty."

She registered a home-based business licence through the Saudi Ministry of Commerce's platform , a process that took three weeks online, far easier than she expected. With an initial investment of just 5,000 SAR for ingredients, packaging, and a dedicated Instagram page, Mama Fatima's Sweets was born. Within six months, she was earning 8,000 SAR per month. By the end of the first year, it had grown to 15,000 SAR , more than many full-time jobs.

"The expat community in Riyadh is hungry for authentic home cooking," she explains. "People miss the flavours of their home countries. I filled a gap that no restaurant could fill."

Today, Fatima supplies three cafés in Riyadh and ships to Jeddah and Dammam through a local courier. The business has given her not only income but identity. "I came to Saudi as Ahmed's wife. Now I am known as 'the kunafa lady' in our compound. That changes how you see yourself."

How the Children Adapted and Thrived

The children's journey was perhaps the most dramatic transformation. For the first year, both children attended international school but struggled to integrate. By year two, something clicked.

"My son started speaking with a Saudi accent , he would say 'Shlonak' instead of 'Izayak,'" Fatima laughs. "At first it bothered me. I felt like I was losing my children to a foreign culture. But then I realised: they were not losing anything. They were gaining a second identity."

The Saudi education system, particularly in international schools, offers a rich extracurricular programme. Both children joined the school football team, a Quran memorisation club, and an annual Science Fair that became a family event. The eldest daughter won second place in the school-wide debate competition on "Saudi Vision 2030 and Youth" , a topic that would have seemed impossible for her to discuss two years earlier.

According to WHO, Research supports what Ahmed and Fatima observed. A 2021 study published in the Journal of International Migration and Integration found that children of expat families in Gulf countries typically show full linguistic and social adaptation within 18 to 24 months, with those under 12 adapting significantly faster than adolescents. The key factors are quality of school environment, parental attitude, and exposure to local peers.

Saudi Vision 2030: Opportunities for Expats

The family's story cannot be told without understanding the broader transformation reshaping Saudi Arabia. Vision 2030, launched in 2016 by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, has fundamentally changed the expat experience in the Kingdom.

Key changes that directly affected this family include:

  • The Premium Residency programme: Launched in 2019, this allows expats to live in Saudi without a sponsor, pay a fixed annual fee, and own property. Ahmed and Fatima are now exploring this option.
  • The Family Visit Visa expansion: Family members can now visit more easily, reducing the isolation that older expat generations experienced.
  • Entertainment city openings: Riyadh Season, Diriyah Season, and Jeddah Season have brought concerts, restaurants, and cultural festivals that make family life richer.
  • Women's empowerment: The lifting of the driving ban in 2018, increased workforce participation, and streamlined business licensing for women have been transformative for expat wives like Fatima.
  • Tourism visas: The introduction of tourist visas means relatives can visit without needing a family visit sponsorship , a small change with enormous emotional impact.

According to data from the Saudi Ministry of Investment, expatriate remittances from Saudi Arabia totalled over 150 billion SAR in 2024, and the expat population stood at approximately 13.4 million. The Kingdom is no longer a place where expats simply earn and leave. It is increasingly a place where families put down roots.

10 Tips for a Smooth Transition to Saudi Arabia

Based on their experience , and conversations with dozens of expat families across Riyadh, Jeddah, and Dammam , Ahmed and Fatima offer these practical tips:

  1. Choose your compound carefully. A good compound with a pool, playground, and community hall will be your lifeline in the first year. Visit at least three before deciding.
  2. Complete your document attestation before you arrive. Getting degrees, marriage certificates, and birth certificates attested in your home country costs a fraction of doing it in Saudi.
  3. Arrive between September and November. The weather is mild, schools have just started, and you have three months to settle before the summer heat returns.
  4. Join WhatsApp groups immediately. Search "Riyadh expat families," "Riyadh moms," or your compound's community group before you even land.
  5. Enrol children in a school with a robust ESL programme. Even if your child speaks English, the Arabic-medium environment in local schools can be challenging.
  6. Open a bank account and get an Absher account on day one. Absher is the government services portal and will be needed for everything from visa renewals to traffic fine payments.
  7. Accept that the first three months will be lonely. Do not panic. Plan for it. Schedule regular video calls with family back home and set a date for your first visit home.
  8. Learn basic Saudi Arabic phrases. You do not need to be fluent, but saying "Yallah," "Shlonak," and "Maa salama" opens doors.
  9. Explore your city on weekends. Riyadh has incredible places , Edge of the World, Diriyah, the National Museum, and the Kingdom Centre sky bridge. Treat each weekend as a mini-adventure.
  10. Consider a home-based business. The regulatory environment for small businesses has improved dramatically. A spouse's hobby can become a meaningful income stream.

Long-Term Settlement: Renting, Schools, and the Future

By year three, Ahmed and Fatima faced a decision that most expat families in Saudi eventually confront: do we stay or go?

The question has multiple dimensions. Financially, Saudi offers a compelling case. The absence of income tax, subsidised utilities (water and electricity are significantly cheaper than in Egypt or most of Europe), and education allowances make it possible to save 30, 40% of household income , something almost impossible in most home countries.

But the decision is not purely financial. "We asked ourselves: where do our children have better opportunities?" Ahmed reflects. "In Egypt, the quality of education in public schools has declined. Private international schools in Cairo cost as much as those in Riyadh but without the same infrastructure. For university, our children can apply to KAUST, King Saud University, or universities abroad with the international curriculum they are studying."

Property ownership became a topic of serious discussion. Under Saudi law, expatriates can own property in specific areas with approval from the Ministry of Justice, provided they hold a valid residence permit. Ahmed and Fatima are now looking at a villa in the Al-Narjis district of Riyadh, priced at 1.2 million SAR. The monthly mortgage payment through a Murabaha (Islamic financing) arrangement would be comparable to their current annual rent of 90,000 SAR , making ownership financially sensible if they commit to staying another five years.

The Premium Residency programme has entered their calculations as well. At 800,000 SAR for the permanent option or 100,000 SAR annually for the renewable one, it offers the security of not being tied to an employer's sponsorship. For a family that has built a life, a business, and a community, that independence is priceless.

Saudi vs. UAE: How the Expat Experience Differs

Both Ahmed and Fatima have friends who chose Dubai or Abu Dhabi instead of Riyadh. The comparison is inevitable for anyone considering the Gulf.

"Dubai is easier in the beginning," Ahmed acknowledges. "The English is universal, the bureaucracy is smoother, the social life is more mixed. But Dubai is also more expensive, more transient, and , this matters to us as a Muslim family , less aligned with the values we want our children to grow up with."

The Takeaway: Building a Home Away from Home

Five years after that anxious flight from Cairo, Ahmed and Fatima's living room in Riyadh looks like any family home anywhere. Photos of the children at school events line the walls. The smell of Fatima's freshly baked kunafa fills the air. The sound of neighbours stopping by for evening tea is as predictable as the adhan.

"I never thought I would say this," Fatima admits, "but Saudi Arabia feels more like home now than Egypt does. Not because Egypt is not home. Because this is where we chose to build our lives. And something about choosing , about working for it, struggling through it, and finally belonging , makes a place yours in a way that birthplace never can."

For every expat family considering the move, the message is this: the first year will be hard. The bureaucracy will test you. The heat will exhaust you. The loneliness will make you question everything. But if you push through , if you find your community at the mosque, the school, and the neighbourhood coffee shop , Saudi Arabia may surprise you with how deeply it can become home.

Vision 2030 has opened a door that was once barely a crack. Families like Ahmed and Fatima's are walking through it every day. And they are building not just careers, but lives.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Saudi Arabia good for expat families?

Yes. Saudi Arabia has become increasingly family-friendly under Vision 2030. Expat compounds offer secure housing, international schools are widely available, and the healthcare system is high-quality. The culture is conservative but welcoming to families who respect local customs.

Can expats own property in Saudi Arabia?

Yes, under certain conditions. Expatriates can own property in Saudi Arabia if they hold a valid residence permit (iqama) and the property is for personal use. Foreign ownership is restricted to specific areas and requires approval from the Ministry of Justice. Most expats still prefer long-term leases.

How is the school system for expat children?

Excellent. Saudi Arabia has a wide network of international schools offering British, American, Indian, and other curricula. Many are in expat compounds or major city districts. Fees range from 30,000 to 100,000 SAR per year depending on the curriculum and facilities.

What is the cost of living for an expat family in Riyadh?

A family of four in Riyadh typically spends 15,000 to 25,000 SAR per month including rent, schooling, utilities, transport, and groceries. Housing is the biggest cost (45,000 to 150,000 SAR per year). Utilities are subsidised and affordable for residents.

How long does it take to feel at home in Saudi?

Most expat families report feeling settled within 6 to 12 months. The first three months are the hardest due to heat, bureaucracy, and homesickness. After finding a community through work, school, or mosque, the adjustment speeds up significantly.
