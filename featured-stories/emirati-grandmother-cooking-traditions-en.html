An Emirati Grandmother Teaches Her Grandchildren Traditional Cooking Every Friday

Jun 16, 2026 8 min read

👩‍🍳 An Emirati Grandmother Teaches Her Grandchildren Traditional Cooking Every Friday

Every Friday in Abu Dhabi, Umm Saeed's home transforms into a noisy kitchen filled with laughter and the aroma of traditional spices. At seven in the morning, six grandchildren aged 4 to 14 line up next to their grandmother, each wearing a small apron with their name on it. This scene is not just family cooking. It is a complete classroom for passing down traditions, values, and life skills. What started as a simple idea three years ago has become a family tradition everyone eagerly awaits.

How the Tradition Started

Umm Saeed, a grandmother in her sixties from Abu Dhabi, noticed that her grandchildren were growing distant from the traditional Emirati kitchen. Fast food and restaurants had replaced home cooking, and the recipes she learned from her own grandmother fifty years ago were at risk of being lost. Umm Saeed recalls: "One day I asked my grandson Hamad: What do you like to eat? He said: Burger. I asked: How about jareesh? He said: What is jareesh? That is when I realized something important was slipping away." At that moment, she decided to take action.

During one family gathering, she told her grandchildren: "Come, let us make luqaimat and jareesh the way my grandmother used to make for me." The children responded with enthusiasm, and Umm Saeed decided to make every Friday a fixed cooking session with the grandchildren. She says: "I chose Friday because it is a day of rest and family. No school, no work. The time is all ours."

The sessions started simply: one delicious dish at a time, then evolved to include the story behind each recipe and its origin, then grew to cover a full weekly meal. Over time, each grandchild developed a fixed role: the oldest helps with cutting under her supervision, the youngest washes vegetables and sets the table. Umm Saeed describes this tradition as "the most important investment of her life." She says: "What I teach my grandchildren today will stay with them long after I am gone. Cooking is just the tool. The real value is connection, love, and tradition."

What the Grandchildren Learned from Their Grandmother

Cooking is not the only skill the grandchildren are gaining. Every Friday, they learn profound life lessons:

  • Patience: Waiting for dough to rise and rice to cook on a low flame. Hamad (12 years) says: "Grandma teaches me that the fastest dish is not always the tastiest."
  • Identity: Each recipe carries a story. Jareesh was the food of farmers in the past, luqaimat is made for celebrations, thareed is linked to holidays. The grandchildren learn not just cooking, but their family history and national heritage.
  • Math: Measuring ingredients, doubling recipes, dividing quantities. Umm Saeed noticed improvement in the grandchildren's math skills since they started cooking with her.
  • Teamwork: Each child has their task, and they learn that delicious food requires cooperation. No one person can do everything alone.
  • Confidence: When the whole family eats from their cooking, the grandchildren feel pride and accomplishment, which builds lasting self-confidence.

The Benefits of a Weekly Family Tradition

This tradition is not just a fun activity. Studies show that cooking with children improves healthy eating behavior and reduces food refusal by up to 30% (World Health Organization, 2025). Children who cook with their families are more likely to try new foods and develop a positive relationship with eating. A study from UAE University (2026) found that children who participate in weekly family cooking consume 25% more vegetables compared to their peers.

Family cooking also strengthens bonds significantly. In an age of screens and constant busyness, a cooking session provides an opportunity for real conversation and interaction away from digital distractions. Umm Saeed says her grandchildren open their hearts while cooking, telling her about school, friends, and interests. "When hands are busy, the tongue flows," she laughs. Her daughter noticed an improvement in her relationship with her own children after she started applying the same idea at home.

There are also unexpected academic benefits. Cooking teaches math in a practical way: measuring ingredients, converting units, doubling quantities. It also improves reading skills through following written recipes. Umm Saeed says: "Hamad used to hate math at school. Now he calculates the rice and spice quantities himself. He applies math without feeling it." This type of experiential learning has proven more effective in improving understanding and information retention compared to traditional theoretical learning.

Children who cook regularly also develop essential life skills: time management (preparing multiple dishes simultaneously), advance planning (creating shopping lists), and problem solving (what to do if the rice burns). These skills are not taught in classrooms but are gained through practical hands-on experience in a safe, supportive environment.

Why Family Cooking Matters Now More Than Ever

In an increasingly fast-paced world where ready meals dominate and restaurants are on every corner, family cooking is cultural and health resistance. Gulf families today face a real challenge in preserving culinary heritage. A Gulf Health Organization report (2026) links the decline in home cooking to rising obesity and diabetes rates among children. Reviving family cooking is an investment in children's health and cultural identity.

The numbers are alarming. According to the World Health Organization, 35% of children in Gulf countries are overweight, and regular home cooking reduces calorie intake by up to 40% compared to eating from restaurants. When children cook with their families, they learn about real food ingredients and develop nutritional awareness that stays with them for life. Umm Saeed confirms: "My grandson Hamad now knows the difference between healthy and unhealthy fats. He never had this awareness before."

Beyond health, there is the psychological dimension. Weekly cooking sessions create a safe space for conversation and family interaction away from screens. Umm Saeed noticed that her grandchildren talk about their problems and fears while cooking, perhaps because busy hands relax the tongue. "Fatima (8 years) once told me: Grandma, I am upset with my mother. And she started telling me the whole story. If we were sitting in front of the TV, that would never have happened."

Financial savings are also significant. A home-cooked meal for a family of 6 costs around 40-60 AED, while the same meal from a restaurant costs 150-200 AED. Over a month, the difference can exceed 4,000 AED. Umm Saeed laughs: "My grandchildren appreciate money more now. When they cook themselves, they know how much food costs."

How to Start This Tradition in Your Family

You do not need to be a skilled cook. Start with one small step this week:

  1. Pick a fixed day: Friday or Thursday, when no one is in a rush.
  2. Choose a simple dish: Even preparing a salad or making sandwiches together is a great start.
  3. Assign tasks by age: A 4-year-old washes vegetables, a 14-year-old supervises the stove.
  4. Add stories: Every dish has a tale. Tell your children who taught you this recipe and when it was eaten.
  5. Document the moment: Take photos and share with family. It creates memories and reinforces the habit.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

How do I start a weekly cooking tradition with my children?
Start with one simple dish the family loves. Pick a fixed day each week. Begin with easy tasks: washing vegetables, safe cutting, decorating. Over time, increase responsibility. The cooking itself is less important than the quality time you spend together.

At what age can children start learning to cook?
Ages 3-5: very simple tasks like washing fruits and setting the table. Ages 6-9: cutting with safe kitchen scissors, mixing ingredients. Ages 10-13: full dishes under supervision. Ages 14+: preparing complete meals independently.

How do I make cooking fun for my children?
Choose colorful, appealing recipes. Let them pick the dish sometimes. Make the kitchen a place for cooperation, not tension. Praise the effort, not just the result. Add stories: this is how your grandmother used to make it.

What skills do children learn from cooking besides cooking itself?
Responsibility, math, patience, creativity, self-confidence, and family traditions. These are comprehensive life skills that go far beyond the kitchen.

What if my children are not interested in learning to cook?
Do not force them. Start by inviting them to participate in a part they might enjoy: decorating cakes, making their own pizza, choosing a juice flavor. Connect cooking to something they love.

How often should we cook together as a family?
Once a week is enough to make a difference. Consistency matters more than frequency. Even 30 minutes of shared cooking weekly improves family bonds and eating habits.
