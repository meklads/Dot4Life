Saudi Father Turned His Garage Into a Carpentry Workshop to Teach His Sons This Summer

June 17, 2026 8 min read Featured Stories

🪚 A Saudi Father Turned His Garage Into a Workshop to Teach His Sons Real Skills This Summer

The sound of a hand saw cuts through the silence—and for the first time since the summer break began, it drowns out the ping of notifications. In a modest garage in Riyadh, Abu Khaled stands with his three sons, each wearing safety goggles and holding a piece of wood. This scene looks nothing like any previous summer. Two years ago, Abu Khaled's boys spent most of their vacation glued to screens. Today, they're busy making something real with their hands—and they actually look happy.

This isn't just a story about woodworking. It's about how one Saudi father turned a common summer problem—boredom, screens, empty hours—into an opportunity to teach his sons life skills, strengthen their relationship, and build their confidence. The best part? Any parent can try this, even without a garage or fancy tools.

How It Started

Abu Khaled, a government employee in his forties, recalls: "Summer 2024 was the hardest. The house was full of arguments over phones. I'd come home from work and find my kids glued to their screens. I felt like I was losing them." He tried several solutions: short trips, summer camps, board games. But the fixes were temporary, and the boys always ended up back in their rooms with their devices.

One day, while fixing a broken wooden shelf in the garage, he noticed his eldest son (14) watching him with genuine curiosity. "He saw me cutting a piece of wood and asked, 'Dad, can I try?' I said, 'Sure, but carefully.' I never expected him to pick up the saw and get the motion right on his first try."

In that simple moment, an idea was born. What if he turned the garage into a small workshop where his boys could spend part of their day? No video games. No YouTube. Just wood, tools, and real creativity.

The early insight: Today's kids are hungry for something real. The tactile feeling, the sense of accomplishment, working with their hands—these are things screens can never truly replicate, no matter how hard they try.

The Garage Becomes a Workshop

The start was modest. Abu Khaled didn't buy expensive power tools. He started with the basics: a hand saw, a hammer, nails, measuring tape, sandpaper, and scrap wood from a nearby hardware store. Total cost: under $80 (300 SAR). "You don't need to start big. The important thing is to start. Even a small corner in your yard or balcony will do."

He dedicated two hours every morning (9 to 11 AM) for workshop time. At first, the boys came out of curiosity. But as days passed, curiosity turned into genuine interest. The middle son (11) says: "At the beginning, I cut my finger. No wait—I mean I cut the wood wrong. But then I learned. Every day I feel like I'm getting better." He laughs telling the story, but the pride in his voice is unmistakable.

Abu Khaled's rule: "There's no failure in the workshop. Only second attempts. Wood can handle mistakes, unlike screens that don't teach patience." This principle—that mistakes are part of learning—is one of the deepest lessons the children absorb.

Five Unexpected Parenting Lessons (Beyond Carpentry)

Every morning in the workshop, the boys learn far more than woodworking skills:

  1. Patience & Persistence: Carpentry offers no instant gratification. It requires planning, precise measuring, careful cutting. They remade one piece three times before getting it right. Each mistake taught them that the next attempt will be better.
  2. Applied Math: Measuring with a ruler, calculating angles, estimating quantities. Abu Khaled says his son's math teacher noticed improvement in geometry after that summer. It wasn't a coincidence.
  3. Confidence & Pride: When a child finishes a wooden piece—no matter how simple—and hangs it in his room, he feels something no app or game can provide: genuine pride. This builds lasting self-confidence.
  4. Problem Solving: The wood piece won't fit as planned? The original design doesn't work? The workshop teaches kids systematic thinking: identify the problem, analyze it, try a solution, adjust, repeat.
  5. Time With Dad: Abu Khaled says: "Before the workshop, my relationship with my kids was superficial. 'Do your homework,' 'Come eat dinner.' Now, in the workshop, I ask them: 'What do you think we should build?' We discuss, plan, work together. Nothing can replace that time."

Each project took a full week. The boys didn't master any of them on the first try, but every attempt was better than the last. Abu Khaled says: "The most important thing is they learn that quality takes time. The shelf they made in week one? Wobbly. The one in week four? We put it in the living room."

5 Practical Tips for Any Parent to Get Started

  1. You don't need a garage or expensive tools. A small corner in the yard or a service room works. Start with a hand saw, hammer, nails, and recycled wood. Estimated cost: $50–80.
  2. Make it a fixed time. Dedicate 1–2 hours daily at the same time. Routine builds discipline and anticipation. Abu Khaled chose mornings when his boys are most energetic.
  3. Safety first. Safety goggles, gloves, constant supervision with sharp tools. Explain the risks before each tool is used. Confidence comes with safe practice.
  4. Let them learn from mistakes. Don't rush in to correct every error. Sometimes a mistake is the best teacher. Ask: "How do you think we should fix this?" instead of saying "That's wrong, do it this way."
  5. Celebrate the finished product. Every piece a child finishes—even the simplest—should have a place in your home. Hang it, use it, talk about it at dinner. This builds confidence and encourages creativity.

Frequently Asked Questions

What age is appropriate for a child to start using hand tools? ▼

Ages 6–7 can use simple tools under direct supervision: a small hammer, a hand saw with a comfortable grip, sandpaper. Ages 10+ can handle more precise tools with continued supervision. Ages 13+ can use light power tools under direct adult guidance. Never leave a child unsupervised with sharp tools.

How much does it cost to set up a simple home workshop? ▼

Between $50 and $130 (200–500 SAR) for the basic setup: hand saw ($8–15), hammer ($5), nails and wood glue ($10), measuring tape ($4), sandpaper ($5), and scrap wood (often free or cheap from hardware stores). Start small and add tools as your children progress.

What are alternatives to woodworking if I don't have garage space? ▼

No outdoor space? Try: cardboard engineering (clean, safe, and free), sewing and embroidery (fits any room), pottery and clay (develops fine motor skills), wood painting (no cutting needed), or complex building sets like technical LEGO. The key isn't the activity itself—it's the principle of regular hands-on work away from screens.

How do I get my kids interested if they're not excited? ▼

Don't force them. Start by making something yourself—fix something, build something simple—and let their curiosity do the work. Leave safe tools within reach. Connect the project to something they love: "Let's build a custom phone stand" or "Let's make a toy for your sister." Choose a simple project they can finish in one session to build confidence.

What are the biggest benefits of teaching kids a hands-on skill? ▼

Multiple proven benefits: naturally reduces screen time (kids are busy with something they love), builds self-confidence through tangible achievement, improves fine motor skills and hand-eye coordination, develops patience and problem-solving, strengthens parent-child bonds through shared work, and teaches kids the value of manual skills. According to Abu Khaled, the best outcome is changing how kids see summer vacation—from "time to kill" to "time to make something we're proud of."
