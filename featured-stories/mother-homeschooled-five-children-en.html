A Mother Who Homeschooled Five Children: A Story of Sacrifice and Barakah

Featured StoryParenting & Education14-year journey

The morning light in Jeddah spills through the kitchen window at exactly 5:47 a.m. in late October. Umm Khalid knows this because for fourteen years she has been awake before that light. By the time the first ray touches the tiles, she has already made her Fajr prayer, drunk a glass of water, and laid out five notebooks on the dining table. Today, like every day, she will teach five children in her own home — from a first-grader learning the Arabic alphabet to a teenager tackling calculus.

This is not a school. This is a home. And Umm Khalid is not a teacher by training. She is a mother who, one difficult evening fourteen years ago, made a decision that changed the course of her family's life.

The Decision to Homeschool

It began with a seven-year-old boy coming home from school with hollow eyes. Khalid, her eldest, had always loved learning. He used to drag his father to the bookstore on Thursdays and spend hours flipping through children's encyclopedias. But after six months in the local private school, something in him had dimmed. He stopped asking questions. He stopped bringing home drawings. When she asked him what he learned that day, he shrugged and said, "Nothing."

Umm Khalid sat with that word for weeks. "Nothing." A child who once devoured knowledge like air was now telling her that school left him empty. She visited the school. The classroom had thirty-two children. The teacher, kind but exhausted, moved through the curriculum like a train that could not stop for passengers who fell behind. Khalid was not falling behind academically — he was ahead in reading — but he was disappearing.

She discussed it with her husband over many nights. He was worried — they both were. Homeschooling was not common in the Gulf at that time. People would talk. Relatives would question. And she had four younger children at home already. How could she teach five children when she had never taught a single one formally?

"I prayed istikhara for three nights," she recalls. "Then I woke up one morning and knew. Not because I was ready. Because I could not send my son back to a place that was dimming his light. The barakah would come later. I just had to start."

— Umm Khalid, reflecting on her decision

She pulled Khalid out of school mid-semester. Word spread through the extended family within days. Her mother called, worried. Her sister-in-law sent a long voice note about socialization and exams and "what about university?" Umm Khalid listened to all of it. Then she sat down at her dining table with a blank notebook and wrote a plan.

The Daily Routine

By the third year, Umm Khalid's homeschool had found its rhythm. Here is a typical day:

6:30 a.m. — Fajr and Quran. The children pray together, then recite their daily Quran memorization. Each child has a small personal goal: half a page for the youngest, two pages for the oldest. Umm Khalid learned tajweed alongside her children, listening to audio recordings at night after they slept.

7:30 a.m. — Breakfast and chores. Every child has a responsibility. The eight-year-old sets the table. The twelve-year-old washes the dishes. The teenagers help prepare lunch. Umm Khalid believes that education includes the hands, not just the mind.

8:30 a.m. — Core subjects. Mathematics, Arabic language, English, and science. She teaches the younger two first while the older three study independently, then rotates. By midday, the dining table is a quiet workshop of concentration.

12:30 p.m. — Dhuhr prayer and break. The children run in the courtyard, climb the small palm tree in the backyard, argue over a board game. Umm Khalid sits on the kitchen floor for ten minutes. Alone. She drinks her tea while it is still hot. "That ten minutes saved my sanity more than any parenting book," she says with a laugh.

2:00 p.m. — Islamic studies and project work. This is her favourite part of the day. She teaches seerah (the Prophet's biography), fiqh (Islamic jurisprudence), and Arabic grammar through stories. The children don't memorise rules — they live inside stories. When they studied the battle of Badr, the ten-year-old built a cardboard map of the battlefield. When they studied the life of Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal, the thirteen-year-old wrote a short play and performed it for the family after dinner.

4:00 p.m. — Afternoon wind-down. The younger ones nap or draw. The older ones read. Umm Khalid uses this time to prepare for the next day. She reads ahead in the textbooks, writes lesson notes, and prints worksheets on the old printer that jams every third page.

Curriculum Choices: Islamic Studies and Core Subjects

One of the hardest questions every homeschooling parent faces is: what do we teach? Umm Khalid chose a blended approach. For core subjects like mathematics and science, she used the Saudi national curriculum as a foundation, supplemented by international resources. For English, she used Cambridge curriculum materials that she found online and adapted.

But the heart of her homeschool was always Islamic studies. "I wanted my children to know why they were Muslims, not just that they were Muslims," she explains. She used the Ministry of Education's Islamic studies textbooks but went far beyond them. She read out loud from Riyad al-Salihin at breakfast. She taught fiqh by acting out wudu (ablution) with the youngest child as her student. When her daughter asked about hijab at age nine, she didn't give a lecture — she told the story of Fatima bint Muhammad ﷺ and let her daughter sit with the question for weeks.

Science happened in the kitchen. Chemistry was baking soda and vinegar volcanoes. Biology was the lemon tree in the backyard, the ants that marched across the terrace after rain, and the unexpected pregnancy of the family cat — which became a six-week unit on mammalian reproduction that none of the children has ever forgotten.

Challenges and How She Overcame Them

It would be dishonest to tell this story without the hard parts. There were days Umm Khalid cried in the bathroom. Days when the children fought and nothing she tried worked. Days when she looked at Facebook posts of other mothers dropping their children at school and felt a sharp, aching envy.

Self-doubt. In the early years, she constantly questioned herself. "Am I teaching them enough? Am I teaching them correctly? What if they fall behind?" She found an answer in an unlikely place: a local homeschooling group on WhatsApp. There were about forty mothers in the group, most of them Saudi and Emirati. They shared resources, vented about tough days, and celebrated small victories. "That group was my lifeline," she says. "It reminded me that I was not alone."

Social pressure. Relatives continued to ask when the children would "go to real school." Her mother-in-law was especially vocal. "She told me I was ruining the children's future," Umm Khalid remembers quietly. "I thanked her for her concern and kept going. Eventually, when the children started excelling academically and socially, the questions stopped."

Financial strain. Umm Khalid's husband worked as an engineer. His salary was enough for their needs, but buying curriculum materials, educational games, and books added unexpected costs. She became resourceful. She borrowed textbooks from other homeschooling families. She found free resources online — Khan Academy, YouTube educational channels, Arabic-language science websites. She learned to print in black-and-white and to share one tablet among three children.

Burnout. By year seven, Umm Khalid was exhausted. She was teaching five children at five different levels, running the household, and managing the emotional load of being "everything" to everyone. Her body gave her signals she ignored until she couldn't. A persistent headache. Sleeplessness. Irritability with the children.

She scaled back. She enrolled the eldest in an online tutoring program for mathematics, one subject only. She hired a university student twice a week to teach English conversation. "I learned that asking for help is not failure," she says. "It is wisdom."

The Children Today

The proof, as they say, is in the fruit. Today, Umm Khalid's five children are young adults and teenagers:

Khalid, now 22, is in his final year of engineering at a respected Saudi university. He graduated from the homeschool ranked first in his cohort on the national equivalency exam. When asked what he remembers most about being homeschooled, he says: "The lunch table. We talked about everything at that table. I learned more about life sitting there than I did from any textbook."

Mariam, 20, is studying Islamic studies at university with plans to become a teacher — a homeschooling mother, she says with a smile. She credits her mother's example for her own love of learning. "I watched her struggle and keep going. That taught me more than any lesson she ever planned."

Yusuf, 17, is preparing for university entrance exams. He wants to study medicine. He was the most resistant to homeschooling as a child — he begged to go to school in fifth grade. Umm Khalid let him try it for one semester. He came back after three months. "The teacher didn't let us ask questions," he said. "I missed our dining table."

Fatima, 14, and Amina, 11, are still being homeschooled. Fatima is working on a small novel in Arabic. Amina is obsessed with astronomy and has a star chart taped to her bedroom ceiling.

What She Would Tell Other Mothers

Today, Umm Khalid sits at the same dining table where she taught five children for fourteen years. The oldest notebooks have been cleared away. The younger ones' books are stacked neatly by the wall. She is quieter now, her voice carrying the weight of someone who has done something difficult and seen it through.

"If you are a mother thinking about homeschooling," she says slowly, choosing each word, "do not wait until you feel ready. You will never feel ready. Start small. Start with one subject. Start with one child for one hour. And trust that Allah will put barakah in the time you give."

She pauses. "And be gentle with yourself. You are not a school. You are a home. A home does not need to be perfect. A home just needs to be there."

Practical Lessons for Homeschooling Families

From fourteen years of trial and error, Umm Khalid distilled the following principles that guided her journey:

  • A fixed schedule gives children security. Her children knew what came next, and that predictability reduced resistance. Even on chaotic days, keeping the morning routine anchored the day.
  • Teach the child, not the curriculum. She adjusted the pace constantly. When a child was struggling, she slowed down. When a child was bored, she accelerated. The curriculum served the child, not the other way around.
  • Include the father. Her husband took the children to the mosque for Isha prayer every night and led weekend field trips — to the science museum, the Red Sea coast, the old city of Jeddah. His involvement gave the children a second teacher and gave her a partner in the mission.
  • Community matters. The WhatsApp group of homeschooling mothers, the weekend study circles with other families, the shared resources — these were not extras. They were essentials.
  • Prioritize your own spiritual and emotional health. Umm Khalid reads Surah Al-Kahf every Friday and takes a thirty-minute walk alone after Asr prayer. "I cannot pour from an empty cup," she says. "A tired mother cannot teach. A rested mother can."

5 Tips for Starting Your Homeschooling Journey

For mothers who are ready to begin but don't know where, Umm Khalid offers five practical starting points:

  1. Start with one subject. Do not try to recreate a full school day from week one. Pick the subject your child struggles with most or the one they love most, and teach that for one hour a day. Add subjects slowly, month by month.
  2. Find your local or online tribe. Join a homeschooling group, even a small one. The moral support and shared resources will save you countless times.
  3. Build a morning rhythm, not a rigid timetable. A rhythm — pray, eat, recite, study, break — is more forgiving than a minute-by-minute schedule. Children feel the flow without needing to read a clock.
  4. Use what is freely available. The internet is full of high-quality, free educational content: Arabic-language science channels on YouTube, Khan Academy for mathematics, OpenStax for textbooks, local library memberships for reading. You do not need a expensive curriculum to start well.
  5. Give yourself permission to change your mind. If something is not working after a fair trial, let it go. The goal is not to prove a method right. The goal is to educate your child. Those are not always the same thing.

Note: This story is based on a composite of real homeschooling experiences in the Gulf region. "Umm Khalid" is a fictional name representing many mothers who have chosen the path of education at home. Individual experiences, regulations, and resources vary by emirate, governorate, and family circumstance. Always consult local education authorities regarding homeschooling requirements in your area.

Final Thoughts: The Barakah of Time

Umm Khalid told me one more story before I left. On a humid August afternoon, when the air conditioner had broken and the children were restless and she had a headache that thudded behind her eyes, her youngest daughter — then six years old — tugged at her sleeve.

"Mama, are you tired of teaching us?"

Umm Khalid looked at her daughter's face. She could have said yes. She could have said, "I am exhausted. I am weary. I don't know if I can do this another year." But something stopped her.

She pulled the girl onto her lap and said, "No, habibti. I am tired because I stayed up late planning your science lesson. But I am not tired of you. Never of you."

The girl hugged her and ran off to play.

"That moment," Umm Khalid says now, her eyes wet, "that moment was worth every difficult day. The barakah of homeschooling is not in the test scores. It is in the small moments of connection that would never have happened if my children were in someone else's care from morning to afternoon."

She folds her hands on the table. The afternoon call to prayer drifts through the window from a nearby mosque. She smiles.

"I would do it all again. Every tired morning. Every doubtful night. Every single time. Because I got to be there. And being there — that is the barakah."
