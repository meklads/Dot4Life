A Family of 6 Living on 3,000 Riyals: The Real Saudi Story

9 min read

A Family of 6 Living on 3,000 Riyals a Month: The Real Saudi Story

According to WHO, This is not a story of poverty. It is a story of dignity, resourcefulness, and the quiet barakah that descends when a family learns to live within its means. Umm Abdullah, not her real name, agreed to share how she and her husband raised four children in Riyadh on 3,000 Saudi riyals a month. Her voice on the phone was calm and warm. She laughed when I asked if it was hard. "Hard? Yes. Impossible? No. We never went to bed hungry."

The story began five years ago when the husband lost his job in a construction company. For three months, the family lived on savings while he searched for work. He eventually found a job as a delivery driver, but the salary was less than half of what he earned before. The family faced a choice: either maintain their previous lifestyle and accumulate debt, or redesign their entire life around a smaller income. They chose the second path.

"Barakah is not in the amount of money. Barakah is in what Allah makes sufficient for you. We learned that lesson deeply." - Umm Abdullah

The Budget: Where Every Riyal Went

Umm Abdullah put together a monthly budget that was precise to the last riyal. There was no room for impulse purchases, no cushion for luxuries, and no reliance on credit cards. Every expense was planned, negotiated, and tracked. The following table shows how they allocated their limited income across essential categories:

CategoryAmount (SAR)Share of Income
Rent (small two-bedroom apartment in Al-Yarmouk)1,000One third
Food (groceries, cooking from scratch)900Three tenths
Utilities (water, electricity, mobile)300One tenth
Transport (bus plus occasional taxi)200Less than one tenth
Children's school supplies200Less than one tenth
Sadaqah (charity, always set aside)100A small portion
Savings and emergency fund300One tenth

The numbers reveal something remarkable. Even on 3,000 riyals, this family saved 300 riyals a month and gave 100 in charity. "The sadaqah was non-negotiable," Umm Abdullah said. "It was the secret to the barakah. The month we gave, the money stretched further. We never understood how, but we witnessed it every single month."

Meal Planning on a Tight Budget

Umm Abdullah's kitchen was the heart of their survival. She cooked every meal from scratch. No processed food, no takeaways, no soft drinks. Her weekly menu rotated around affordable staples that provided nutrition and satisfaction without breaking the budget.

Kabsa with chicken was their Sunday meal. One chicken fed the whole family when stretched with rice, tomatoes, onions, and spices. The cost per meal was approximately fifteen riyals. Jareesh, a traditional crushed wheat dish, appeared twice a week. It was nutritious, filling, and remarkably cheap. A large bag of crushed wheat lasted two weeks and cost less than ten riyals.

Lentil soup with bread was their protein-rich midweek dinner. Lentils cost five riyals per kilogram. A single kilogram made enough soup for three meals. The bread came from a local bakery for one riyal per loaf. Eggs were versatile and economical. A tray of thirty eggs cost fifteen riyals and appeared in breakfast, lunch, and baking.

"I learned to buy in bulk from the vegetable market on Thursdays," she explained. "The vendors discount late in the day. I would buy damaged boxes of tomatoes for two riyals and make tomato paste that lasted months. I also learned which fruits were in season. Bananas and dates were affordable year-round. Watermelon in summer was a treat that cost almost nothing."

Children's Activities That Cost Nothing

Umm Abdullah's children never attended paid extracurricular classes. Instead, the family created a rich environment at home and in their neighbourhood that cost nothing but time and imagination.

The home library was their first treasure. Second-hand books from charity shops cost between one and five riyals each. The children read aloud to each other, discussed stories, and wrote their own endings. The building's courtyard served as their playground. Games of tag, hide-and-seek, and jump rope provided exercise and social interaction.

Friday trips followed Jumu'ah prayer. The family walked through their neighbourhood, exploring streets they had never noticed. "The children competed to spot the most interesting plants or birds," Umm Abdullah recalled. "They kept journals of what they saw. That was their science class."

The kitchen itself was a classroom. The children helped cook, which taught them mathematics through measuring ingredients, biology through understanding nutrition, and responsibility through completing tasks. Storytelling nights after Isha prayer were the week's highlight. The father told stories from the Quran, Arab folklore, and his own childhood. The children asked questions, debated morals, and fell asleep with lessons embedded in their hearts.

The Role of Community and Extended Family

No family survives alone, and Umm Abdullah's family was no exception. Their community network provided invisible support that never appeared in the budget but made survival possible.

Neighbours shared excess food regularly. When someone cooked too much kabsa, they sent a plate upstairs. When tomatoes were cheap, they bought extra and distributed them. The extended family contributed during Eid, providing new clothes for the children that the parents could not afford. Grandparents took the children for weekends, giving the parents time to rest and plan.

This mutual support was not charity in the negative sense. It was the Islamic practice of takaful, social solidarity, that the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, encouraged. Umm Abdullah also gave what she could. When she baked bread, she sent loaves to neighbours. When she had extra food, she invited others. "Giving does not make you poorer," she said. "It makes you richer in ways money cannot measure."

How They Handled Unexpected Expenses

Life does not follow a budget perfectly. Medical emergencies, school fees, and broken appliances appear without warning. Umm Abdullah's family developed strategies for these moments.

The 300 riyals monthly savings created a small emergency fund. Over a year, this accumulated to 3,600 riyals, enough to cover most minor crises. For larger expenses, they relied on family loans that were repaid without interest, following Islamic principles. They avoided commercial debt completely, which prevented the cycle of interest payments from consuming their limited income.

"The hardest unexpected expense was when my son needed glasses," Umm Abdullah remembered. "The frames and lenses cost 400 riyals. We used two months of savings and borrowed the rest from my brother. It took us four months to repay him, but we did it. That experience taught my son to take care of his glasses, and it taught all of us that we can handle difficulties together."

What This Family Teaches Us

This story is not a prescription for every family, but it holds universal lessons that apply across cultures and income levels.

Cooking from scratch is the single biggest expense saver. Pre-made food costs three to five times more than home-cooked meals. A bag of lentils costs five riyals and feeds a family three times. Setting aside charity even when you have little brings barakah that mathematics cannot explain. Umm Abdullah insists this brought unexpected ease to their lives.

Community matters profoundly. Neighbours share, extended families support, and no family is an island. Children do not need paid activities to thrive. They need attention, imagination, and freedom to play. Simplicity is a choice, not a failure. Umm Abdullah's children are now adults. One is an engineer, another a teacher. They look back on those years not as deprivation, but as their richest time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it really possible to support a family of 6 on 3,000 SAR in Riyadh?

It is extremely tight and requires near-perfect discipline. Rent is the biggest challenge, as a decent two-bedroom apartment in an affordable area costs between 800 and 1,200 SAR. Food costs require cooking from scratch and bulk buying. This family succeeded through strict budgeting, community support, and a mindset of contentment. For most families today, 4,000 to 5,000 SAR is a more realistic minimum.

What was the hardest part of living on such a tight budget?

The social pressure was the hardest part. Seeing other families buy new clothes for Eid, go on trips, and own cars. The family taught their children that worth is not measured by possessions. The parents themselves had to unlearn years of comparing themselves to others.

How did the family handle unexpected expenses?

They kept 300 riyals as emergency savings every month. For larger unexpected costs, they relied on their extended family and community support networks. They also avoided debt completely, which prevented the cycle of interest payments from consuming their limited income.

What advice would Umm Abdullah give to struggling families today?

Do not compare your life to others. Your path is yours alone. Involve your children in cooking and budgeting so they understand that financial difficulty is temporary. Never cut charity, even if it is only 5 riyals. And trust Allah completely, because He is the Provider.

Did the children's education suffer from the limited budget?

No. The children attended public schools, used second-hand books, and learned practical skills at home. Two of the children are now professionals, an engineer and a teacher. They look back on those years as their richest time, not as deprivation.
