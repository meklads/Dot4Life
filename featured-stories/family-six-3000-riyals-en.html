A Family of 6 Living on 3,000 Riyals a Month: The Real Saudi Story

This is not a story of poverty. It is a story of dignity, resourcefulness, and the quiet barakah that descends when a family learns to live within its means. Umm Abdullah — not her real name — agreed to share how she and her husband raised four children in Riyadh on 3,000 Saudi riyals a month. Her voice on the phone was calm, warm. She laughed when I asked if it was hard: "Hard? Yes. Impossible? No. We never went to bed hungry."

"Barakah is not in the amount of money. Barakah is in what Allah makes sufficient for you. We learned that lesson deeply." — Umm Abdullah

The numbers reveal something striking: even on 3,000 riyals, this family saved 300 riyals a month and gave 100 in charity. "The sadaqah was non-negotiable," Umm Abdullah said. "It was the secret to the barakah. The month we gave, the money stretched further."

Meal Planning on a Tight Budget

Umm Abdullah's kitchen was the heart of their survival. She cooked every meal from scratch — no processed food, no takeaways, no soft drinks. Her weekly menu rotated around affordable staples:

  • Kabsa with chicken: One chicken fed the whole family. Rice, tomatoes, onions, and spices filled the pot. Cost per meal: ~15 SAR.
  • Jareesh: A traditional crushed wheat dish. Nutritious, filling, cheap. A bag of crushed wheat lasted two weeks.
  • Lentil soup + bread: Protein-rich, inexpensive. Served with whole wheat bread from the local bakery (1 SAR per loaf).
  • Eggs: Used in multiple ways — scrambled, in shakshuka, boiled for school lunches. A tray of 30 eggs cost 15 SAR.
  • Fruit: Bananas and local dates were the most affordable. Watermelon in summer.

"I learned to buy in bulk from the vegetable market on Thursdays," she explained. "The vendors discount late in the day. I would buy damaged boxes of tomatoes and make tomato paste that lasted months."

Children's Activities That Cost Nothing

Umm Abdullah's children never attended paid extracurricular classes. Instead:

  • Home library: Second-hand books from charity shops (1–5 SAR each). The children read aloud to each other.
  • Neighbourhood play: The building's courtyard was their playground. Tag, hide-and-seek, jump rope.
  • Friday trips: Walking to the local mosque for Jumu'ah, then a family walk through the neighbourhood. "The children competed to spot the most interesting plants or birds."
  • Kitchen helpers: The children helped cook, which taught them maths (measuring ingredients) and responsibility.
  • Storytelling nights: After Isha prayer, Umm Abdullah's husband would tell stories — from the Quran, from Arab folklore, from his own childhood.

What This Family Teaches Us

This story is not a prescription for every family, but it holds universal lessons:

  1. Cook from scratch. The single biggest expense saver. Pre-made food costs 3–5x more. A bag of lentils costs 5 SAR and feeds a family.
  2. Set aside charity even when you have little. Umm Abdullah insists this brought the barakah. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Charity does not decrease wealth."
  3. Community matters. Neighbours shared excess food. The extended family contributed during Eid. No family is an island.
  4. Children do not need paid activities to thrive. They need attention, imagination, and the freedom to play.
  5. Simplicity is a choice, not a failure. Umm Abdullah's children are now adults — one is an engineer, another a teacher. They look back on those years not as deprivation, but as their richest time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it really possible to support a family of 6 on 3,000 SAR today in Riyadh?

It is extremely tight and requires near-perfect discipline. Rent is the biggest challenge — a decent 2-bedroom apartment in an affordable area costs 800-1,200 SAR. Food costs require cooking from scratch and bulk buying. This family succeeded through strict budgeting, community support, and a mindset of contentment. For most families, 4,000-5,000 SAR is a more realistic minimum today.

What was the hardest part?

"The social pressure," Umm Abdullah admitted. "Seeing other families buy new clothes for Eid, go on trips, own a car. The hardest thing was not feeling less than them. We taught our children that our worth is not in what we own."

What advice would Umm Abdullah give to families struggling financially today?

"First, do not compare. Your path is yours. Second, involve your children — let them see the budget, help cook, understand that financial difficulty is temporary. Third, never cut sadaqah. Even 5 riyals. It opens doors you cannot see. And fourth, trust Allah. He is Ar-Razzaq — The Provider."
