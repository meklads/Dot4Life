My Year Without Social Media: A Father's Story

It started on a Tuesday. I was sitting at the dinner table with my family — my wife, our three children — and I realized I had no idea what my eldest son had said for the last five minutes. I was scrolling. Not because anything urgent was happening on my feed, but because scrolling had become the default position of my hands.

I put the phone face-down on the table. I looked at my son. He was looking at me — waiting. I had missed his question. I apologized and asked him to repeat it. He did, but the moment had passed. The connection was broken.

That night, I made a decision: I would delete every social media app from my phone for one year. Not a detox. Not a digital fast. A year. 365 days. I told my wife. She smiled — the kind of smile that says "I'll believe it when I see it."

The First Month: Withdrawal

The first two weeks were surprisingly hard. I found myself reaching for my phone dozens of times a day, only to find a blank grid where Instagram used to be. I felt anxious, disconnected, almost lonely — even though I was surrounded by my family. I realized I had been using social media not for connection, but for escape. Escape from what? From the quiet moments, the pauses, the presence.

The Gift of Boredom

By month three, something shifted. Without the constant drip of notifications, my mind began to settle. I started to notice things: the way the light falls in our living room at 4 PM, the specific laugh my daughter makes when she's truly amused, the sound of my wife humming while cooking. These had always been there. I had just been too distracted to notice.

The Year That Changed Everything

I won't claim that quitting social media solved all our problems. It didn't. But it created space for other things: conversations that lasted longer than a comment thread, evenings where we played games instead of watching separate screens, a slow but steady reconnection with my own thoughts.

When the year ended, I did not reinstall the apps. My wife stopped smiling that smile. And at dinner now, I don't always know what my son is saying — some days I still drift. But I catch myself faster. And I put the phone down. For good.
