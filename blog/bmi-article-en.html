BMI Article: The Problem with BMI and a Better Alternative

2026-06-088 min read

Salman Story: Why BMI Alone Is Not Enough for Gulf Families

Salman, a 42-year-old father of three from Riyadh, visited his doctor for an annual checkup. He weighed 78 kg and his height was 172 cm, giving him a BMI of 26.4, which is classified as "overweight." The doctor told him his weight was slightly above normal and suggested he lose a few kilograms. Salman left the clinic feeling mildly concerned but not alarmed. However, a deeper health assessment told a different story: his waist circumference was 98 cm, his fasting blood sugar was elevated at 6.4 mmol/L, and his liver enzymes were above normal. Despite having a BMI that was only slightly elevated, Salman had metabolic syndrome: excess visceral fat, insulin resistance, and early-stage fatty liver disease. This is the BMI paradox that affects millions of Gulf residents. According to research cited by the International Diabetes Federation, people of Middle Eastern and South Asian descent develop metabolic complications at lower BMI thresholds than European populations. A BMI of 25 in a Gulf resident may carry the same metabolic risk as a BMI of 30 in a European person. This means that relying solely on BMI can give a false sense of security to millions of families in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman who think they are healthy because their BMI is "normal" or only slightly elevated.

What Is BMI and How Is It Calculated?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a simple calculation that divides your weight in kilograms by the square of your height in meters. For example, a person weighing 78 kg with a height of 1.72 m has a BMI of 78 divided by (1.72 times 1.72) which equals 26.4. The World Health Organization classifies BMI into categories: below 18.5 is underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 is normal weight, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 or above is obese. BMI was developed in the 1830s by Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet and was never intended as a health diagnostic tool. It was designed to measure population-scale body mass patterns, not to assess individual health. Despite this, it became the standard screening tool for weight-related health risk worldwide because it is simple, free, and requires no special equipment. However, its limitations are significant, especially for non-European populations. BMI does not distinguish between muscle and fat, does not account for fat distribution, and uses cutoffs developed primarily from European data. For Gulf residents, these limitations are not just academic, they have real consequences for health screening and early intervention.

Why BMI Is Not Enough: 5 Critical Limitations

Understanding the limitations of BMI is essential for accurate health assessment. First, BMI cannot distinguish between muscle and fat. A muscular athlete may have a high BMI but very low body fat, while a sedentary person may have a normal BMI but high body fat percentage. Second, BMI does not measure fat distribution. Visceral fat stored around the organs is far more dangerous than subcutaneous fat, and BMI completely misses this distinction. Third, BMI cutoffs were developed from European populations and do not account for ethnic differences in body composition. Studies show that Middle Eastern and Asian populations have higher body fat percentages at lower BMI values. Fourth, BMI does not capture metabolic health. A person with normal BMI can have insulin resistance, high blood pressure, or abnormal cholesterol, a condition called metabolically obese normal weight (MONW). Fifth, BMI changes with age. Muscle mass naturally decreases after age 30, meaning BMI may underestimate fat percentage in older adults. Gulf-Specific Factors That Affect BMI Accuracy

Several factors make BMI less accurate for Gulf residents specifically. Genetic predisposition: people of Middle Eastern and South Asian descent tend to store more visceral fat at lower BMI levels. Many clinicians note that adults in the Gulf may show metabolic risk patterns at lower BMI levels than some European populations. Dietary patterns: the traditional Gulf diet, while rich in nutritious elements, has shifted in recent decades toward high consumption of refined carbohydrates, sugary drinks, and fried foods, contributing to metabolic syndrome even in individuals with normal BMI. Physical activity levels: extreme summer heat in Gulf countries limits outdoor physical activity for much of the year, reducing muscle mass and lowering calorie expenditure. According to the Saudi Ministry of Health, less than 30 percent of Saudi adults meet the recommended weekly physical activity levels. Body composition differences: research indicates that at the same BMI, Middle Eastern populations have approximately 3 to 5 percent more body fat than European populations, meaning a Gulf resident with a BMI of 25 may have the same body fat percentage as a European with a BMI of 28.

Better Measures: Waist Circumference, Body Fat, and Waist-to-Height Ratio

Since BMI alone is insufficient, health experts recommend combining multiple measurements for a complete picture. Waist circumference is a simple yet powerful indicator of visceral fat. For Middle Eastern populations, the recommended maximum is 94 cm for men and 80 cm for women. Waist-to-height ratio is even more accurate: keep your waist measurement below half your height. A person 170 cm tall should have a waist below 85 cm. If the ratio exceeds 0.5, health risk begins to climb. Body fat percentage provides the most complete picture of body composition. For Gulf men, healthy body fat ranges between 12 and 20 percent for adults under 40, and between 14 and 22 percent for those over 40. For Gulf women, healthy ranges are 20 to 30 percent under 40 and 22 to 32 percent over 40. These ranges are higher than European recommendations because Gulf populations typically carry slightly more essential fat. The Saudi Commission for Health Specialties recommends using waist circumference alongside BMI for routine health screening in Saudi clinical practice.

How to Improve Your Numbers: Practical Strategies

Improving your BMI, waist circumference, and overall metabolic health requires consistent lifestyle changes rather than quick fixes. The most effective strategies focus on the root causes of metabolic syndrome. First, reduce added sugars and refined carbohydrates. Sugary drinks, sweetened coffees, pastries, and white bread drive visceral fat accumulation and insulin resistance more than any other food group. Replace sugary drinks with water or unsweetened beverages, and replace white rice and bread with whole grain alternatives like bulgur wheat, brown rice, or quinoa in at least half your meals. Second, increase protein intake to preserve muscle mass during weight loss. Protein stimulates satiety hormones and maintains metabolic rate. Aim for 20 to 30 grams of protein per meal from sources like eggs, Greek yogurt, chicken, fish, and legumes. Third, incorporate strength training twice per week. Building muscle improves insulin sensitivity for 24 to 48 hours after each session and directly combats metabolic decline. Bodyweight exercises like squats, pushups, and lunges are effective and require no equipment. Fourth, walk 8,000 to 10,000 steps daily. In Gulf summers, walking in air-conditioned malls or on a treadmill at home is a practical alternative to outdoor walking. Fifth, prioritize sleep: sleeping fewer than 6 hours per night raises cortisol and promotes visceral fat storage. Aim for 7 to 8 hours of quality sleep consistently. Frequently Asked Questions About BMI for Gulf Residents

Is BMI accurate for athletes and people who exercise regularly?

No, BMI is particularly inaccurate for athletes and regularly active individuals because it cannot distinguish between muscle and fat. Muscle is denser than fat, so muscular individuals often have a high BMI despite having very low body fat percentage. For active individuals, body fat percentage and waist circumference are much better health indicators.

What is a healthy BMI for Gulf residents specifically?

Given the metabolic differences in Middle Eastern populations, many health experts recommend adjusted BMI thresholds for Gulf residents. A BMI below 23 is considered optimal, 23 to 25 requires monitoring, and above 25 indicates elevated metabolic risk. These adjusted thresholds better reflect the actual health risks faced by Gulf populations.

Can you have a normal BMI and still be unhealthy?

Yes, this condition is called metabolically obese normal weight (MONW). A person can have a normal BMI but carry excess visceral fat, have insulin resistance, high blood pressure, or abnormal cholesterol. This is particularly common in people of Middle Eastern and South Asian descent. This is why BMI alone is not enough for a complete health assessment.

How often should I measure my BMI and waist circumference?

Measure your BMI and waist circumference once per month to track trends. Weekly measurements can be misleading due to natural fluctuations in water weight and digestion. Focus on the direction of the trend over 3 to 6 months rather than the daily number. Take measurements at the same time of day, preferably in the morning before eating.

What is the most accurate way to measure body fat at home?

The most practical and accurate home method is the combination of waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio. These are free, require only a measuring tape, and correlate strongly with metabolic risk. Body fat scales using bioelectrical impedance are convenient but less accurate, varying by hydration level and time of day. Use them for trend tracking rather than absolute numbers.
