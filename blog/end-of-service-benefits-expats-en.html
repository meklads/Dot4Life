End of Service Benefits Guide for Expats

2026-06-088 min read

Faisal's Story: 18 Years and a Life-Changing Payout

"I had no idea the payout would be this substantial." That is how Faisal, a 45-year-old Pakistani civil engineer, describes receiving his end-of-service benefit after 18 years with a major contracting company in Riyadh. In early 2026, when he decided to return to Pakistan permanently, his employer handed him a check for 250,000 SAR, approximately 66,000 USD. "I knew something was waiting for me after nearly two decades, but I never sat down to calculate the actual number. That money completely changed my family's situation. We bought a house in Islamabad and started a small agriculture business." Faisal's story represents what is possible when an expat maximizes years of service and maintains a proper basic salary structure. His 18 years in Saudi Arabia placed him well past the critical five-year threshold that doubles the annual accrual rate, and his role as a senior engineer meant his basic salary had grown substantially over time. Yet even with this success, Faisal admits he made mistakes: "I should have started my own savings earlier. If I had saved just 10 percent of my monthly income over those 18 years, I would have had twice what I received from the company." This guide will help you understand exactly how your EOS benefit works, what your legal rights are, and how to build a complete financial plan that uses your EOS as a powerful supplement rather than your only safety net.

What Is the End-of-Service Benefit?

The end-of-service (EOS) benefit, also known as gratuity in the UAE, is a mandatory lump-sum payment that employers in Saudi Arabia and the UAE must pay to employees when their employment ends. It applies to resignations, contract completions, and terminations initiated by the employer, though it is forfeited in cases of gross misconduct or termination for cause. The benefit functions as deferred compensation that rewards long service and provides financial security during the transition between jobs or countries. The single most important factor to understand is that the entire calculation is based exclusively on your basic salary, not your total compensation package. Housing allowances, transportation allowances, mobile phone stipends, education allowances, and annual bonuses are all excluded from the EOS calculation. This means that a professional with a 20,000 SAR total package structured as 10,000 SAR basic salary receives only half the EOS of someone whose package is structured as 15,000 SAR basic salary and 5,000 SAR allowances, even though the total take-home pay is identical. Understanding this distinction from the moment you sign your first contract is the foundation of strategic EOS planning.

End-of-Service Benefits in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Labor Law, regulated by the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development (MHRSD), defines a clear two-tier structure for EOS calculation. For the first five years of continuous service, the employee receives half a month of basic salary for each year. For each year beyond five years, the rate doubles to one full month of basic salary per year. Consider an expat with a basic salary of 10,000 SAR who has worked for 10 years. The calculation proceeds as follows: years 1 through 5: 5 years multiplied by 5,000 SAR which equals 25,000 SAR. Years 6 through 10: 5 years multiplied by 10,000 SAR which equals 50,000 SAR. Total EOS entitlement: 75,000 SAR. The jump at year six is dramatic, because the annual accrual rate increases by 100 percent, which makes staying past the five-year mark one of the most financially consequential decisions an expat can make. For resignations, the amount is reduced unless the employee has completed 10 continuous years of service. Between 2 and 5 years of service, the resignation entitlement is two-thirds of the full amount. Between 5 and 10 years, it is also two-thirds of the full amount. Only after 10 full years does the resigning employee receive the entire calculated benefit. The full text of the Saudi Labor Law provides additional details on specific cases including part-time employment, multiple contracts, and termination for medical reasons. The benefit must be paid within two weeks of the termination date, and employers who delay payment face administrative penalties.

End-of-Service Benefits in the UAE

The UAE's end-of-service gratuity system, governed by the Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation (MOHRE), uses a different calculation method based on daily wages. For employees with one to five years of service, the entitlement is 21 days of basic salary for each year. For employees with more than five years of service, the rate increases to 30 days of basic salary for each year. The total gratuity is capped at the equivalent of two years of basic salary. Using the same example of a 10,000 AED basic monthly salary over 10 years, the daily wage is calculated as 10,000 divided by 30, which equals approximately 333 AED per day. The first five years yield 5 multiplied by 21 multiplied by 333, which equals 34,965 AED. The second five years yield 5 multiplied by 30 multiplied by 333, which equals 49,950 AED. The total gratuity is 84,915 AED. The UAE system also distinguishes between limited contracts, which have a fixed end date, and unlimited contracts, which are open-ended. Resignation entitlements differ: for limited contracts, the employee receives the full gratuity after completing one year. For unlimited contracts, the gratuity is reduced if the employee resigns before three years of service. Since 2022, the UAE has offered the Voluntary Alternative End-of-Service Savings Scheme, which allows employers to contribute EOS amounts into regulated investment funds managed by licensed financial institutions. This optional scheme provides additional protection for employees by separating the EOS liability from the employer's balance sheet and potentially generating investment returns on the accumulated amount.

Key Legal Protections Every Expat Should Know

Knowing your legal rights protects you from common underpayment scenarios and helps you take appropriate action if your employer fails to comply. First, the EOS benefit is calculated on basic salary only, a rule that applies in both countries and is the most frequent source of disputes. Second, termination for cause, defined narrowly as gross misconduct, criminal acts, or serious breach of company policies that result in material loss, leads to complete forfeiture of the EOS benefit. Minor disciplinary issues do not qualify as cause. Third, resignation reduces the benefit under specific conditions: in Saudi Arabia, the full amount is only available after 10 years, while in the UAE for unlimited contracts, the full amount is available after 3 years. Fourth, payment deadlines are legally mandated: within two weeks in Saudi Arabia and within 14 days in the UAE. Fifth, dispute resolution channels are well established. In Saudi Arabia, employees can file a complaint through the MHRSD online portal or visit a labor office. In the UAE, complaints can be filed through the MOHRE mobile application or by calling the ministry's contact center. Free zone employees in the UAE should note that their employment may be governed by the regulations of their specific free zone authority rather than the UAE Labor Law. The Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC) and Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM) have their own independent employment frameworks with different EOS provisions.

Five Strategies to Maximize Your Benefit

While the EOS calculation formula is fixed by law, you can take proactive steps to maximize the amount you ultimately receive. First, negotiate basic salary, not total package. When evaluating a job offer, focus on the basic salary component because allowances and bonuses do not affect your EOS. A package of 12,000 SAR basic plus 3,000 SAR allowances generates a significantly larger EOS than 8,000 SAR basic plus 7,000 SAR allowances, even though the total monthly income is identical. Second, understand your contract type before signing. In the UAE, knowing whether you have a limited or unlimited contract allows you to plan your tenure strategically. Third, time your resignation around service milestones. In Saudi Arabia, resigning at 4 years and 11 months instead of 5 years and 1 day costs you the higher accrual rate that applies after the five-year threshold. Waiting just one extra day changes your annual EOS accrual from half a month to one full month. Fourth, keep meticulous records. Save copies of your employment contract, any salary revision letters, and annual leave records. Request a written statement of your accrued EOS benefit every year and verify that the calculation matches the legal formula. Fifth, avoid actions that could lead to termination for cause. Even a single incident of gross misconduct can forfeit years of accumulated benefits, making it essential to maintain professional conduct throughout your employment.

How to Allocate Your End-of-Service Payment

Receiving a five or six-figure lump sum can feel overwhelming, but a structured allocation plan ensures the money serves your long-term financial goals effectively. Financial planners recommend a specific priority order. Start by eliminating high-interest debt, particularly credit card balances and personal loans, which typically carry interest rates between 15 and 30 percent in the Gulf region. Paying off this debt is equivalent to earning a guaranteed return of the same rate. Next, establish an emergency fund of three to six months of living expenses in your home country, held in an accessible savings account. This fund prevents you from having to sell investments at a loss or take on expensive debt if an unexpected expense arises. Third, invest for retirement through a diversified portfolio appropriate for your time horizon. Because Gulf expats do not typically have access to state pension systems, self-funded retirement planning is not optional but essential. Consider low-cost index funds that track global equity and bond markets. Fourth, allocate funds to your children's education if you are returning to a country where school fees or university costs differ from the Gulf. Fifth, use any remaining amount for other long-term goals such as a home down payment, starting a business, or advanced professional education. Why You Need Your Own Savings Plan Alongside EOS

Relying on your EOS benefit as your primary savings vehicle is one of the most common financial mistakes Gulf expats make. Consider the limitations. The average expat's basic salary is only 50 to 70 percent of the total compensation package, meaning the EOS calculation uses a substantially lower figure than the income the employee actually lives on. A professional earning 25,000 SAR total package with a 12,000 SAR basic salary over 15 years would receive approximately 160,000 SAR in EOS, which sounds significant but represents less than one year of total income. Additionally, you may change jobs before accumulating a meaningful benefit because the median tenure for expats in the Gulf is approximately four to six years, which places most workers in the lowest EOS accrual tier. The benefit is not adjusted for inflation, meaning its purchasing power declines every year you work. You have no control over the timing of the payment, and employer non-payment, while legally actionable, can take months to resolve. The solution is clear: build your own independent savings and investment portfolio. Aim to save 10 to 20 percent of your total monthly income in a diversified investment account, as though the EOS benefit did not exist. When your EOS payment eventually arrives, treat it as a strategic bonus that accelerates your existing plan, not as the plan itself. Action Plan

Step 1: Calculate your current EOS entitlement using the formulas for your country of employment. Use your basic salary and years of continuous service.
Step 2: Open a separate investment account and automate monthly transfers equal to 10 percent of your total monthly income. Set up the automatic transfer on payday so you never see the money in your spending account.
Step 3: Request an annual EOS statement from your employer and verify the accrued amount matches the legal formula. Report any discrepancies immediately.
Step 4: Write down your allocation plan for when your EOS payment arrives, specifying exact amounts for debt repayment, emergency fund, and long-term investments. Having a plan before the money arrives prevents impulsive spending.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens to my EOS benefit if I change jobs within the Gulf?

When you leave one employer, they are required to pay your accrued EOS benefit at the time of departure. Your new employer starts the calculation from zero. There is no mechanism to transfer EOS balances between employers in either Saudi Arabia or the UAE. This is why negotiating a higher basic salary with each new employer is critical, because your EOS resets with every job change, so each new basic salary determines your accrual rate for that period.

Is my end-of-service benefit taxable in Saudi Arabia or the UAE?

No. Saudi Arabia and the UAE do not impose personal income tax, and EOS benefits are specifically exempt from any taxation in both countries. However, if you are a citizen of a country that taxes worldwide income, such as the United States, you may need to report the EOS payment on your annual tax return and potentially pay tax depending on your specific circumstances. Consult a tax advisor familiar with expat taxation in your home country.

Can my employer deduct from my EOS benefit?

Deductions are only permitted for specific reasons defined in each country's labor law. These include outstanding loans or advances provided by the employer, damage caused by the employee to company property due to negligence, and unearned annual leave taken in advance. Your employer cannot arbitrarily deduct from your EOS benefit for general business losses, performance issues, or any reason not explicitly listed in the labor law. If you believe deductions are improper, you can file a complaint with the relevant ministry.

How is EOS calculated for free zone employees in the UAE?

Free zone employees in the UAE may be subject to different employment regulations depending on the specific free zone authority. Some free zones, such as the Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC) and Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM), have their own independent labor laws with separate EOS calculation methods. Other free zones follow the UAE Labor Law. Always review your employment contract and the specific free zone regulations that apply to your situation, as the gratuity calculation may differ from the mainland UAE formula.

What should I do if my employer refuses to pay my EOS benefit?

If your employer fails to pay your EOS benefit within the legally mandated timeframe, you have several options. In Saudi Arabia, file a complaint through the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development online portal or visit the nearest labor office. In the UAE, use the MOHRE mobile application or call the ministry's contact center. Both countries have established labor dispute resolution mechanisms that handle EOS claims. Keep all documentation including your employment contract, salary slips, and any written communication with your employer regarding the benefit.

Your end-of-service benefit is a significant financial asset that can accelerate your family's long-term goals, but it should never be your only savings plan. Understand the calculation rules for your country, negotiate your basic salary strategically, build your own independent investment portfolio alongside your employer's contribution, and create a clear allocation plan for when the payment arrives. With proper planning, your EOS benefit becomes a powerful tool that complements, rather than substitutes for, your personal financial discipline. Start today by calculating your current entitlement and setting up an automated savings system that builds your future regardless of your employer's contributions.
