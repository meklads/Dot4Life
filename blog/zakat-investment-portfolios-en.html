Zakat on Investment Portfolios

2026-06-088 min read

Zakat is one of the Five Pillars of Islam, obligatory purification of wealth for every Muslim who possesses wealth above the Nisab threshold. For centuries, the calculations were straightforward: gold, silver, livestock, agricultural produce, trade goods. But the 21st-century Muslim investor holds ETFs, real estate investment trusts, cryptocurrency wallets, and employee stock options. How does Zakat apply to these?

Start with the basic Zakat rules for cash and trade goods, then apply them carefully to ETFs, mutual funds, and portfolios. For general orientation (not a personal fatwa), see the Ministry of Islamic Affairs.

The Two Fundamentals

Nisab: The minimum threshold before Zakat becomes obligatory. Currently equivalent to approximately 85g of gold or 595g of silver. Use whichever is lower (silver Nisab is more protective of the poor, it triggers Zakat earlier). At today's prices, approximately SAR 2,500-3,500 in the GCC.
Hawl: The wealth must have been held for one complete lunar year (354 days). Accumulated above Nisab for the full year, otherwise no Zakat on that portion.

Asset Class Rulings

Listed Stocks (Equities)
Majority ruling: Zakat on zakatable assets of the company, not market value
If you hold shares in a company, you effectively own a proportional share of that company's assets. The scholarly consensus (AAOIFI Standard 35) is that Zakat should be calculated on the zakatable net assets per share, not the stock price. The company's zakatable assets typically include cash, receivables, and inventory. Fixed assets (buildings, equipment) are generally excluded.

Practical shortcut: Many companies publish their Zakat-applicable asset value per share in their annual reports, particularly in GCC countries where corporate Zakat disclosure is standard.
Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)
Ruling: Zakat on received income only, not on the fund's net asset value
REITs primarily hold real property, buildings, land, shopping centres. Property held for rental income (not for trade) is generally exempt from Zakat as an asset class. However, the income distributed by the REIT is subject to Zakat once received and if it has been held for a hawl. The majority view: Zakat = two and a half percent on distributions received and accumulated above Nisab for one year.
Mutual Funds & ETFs
Ruling: Depends on fund type, equity funds follow stock ruling; money market funds pay two and a half percent on total value
For equity-based mutual funds and ETFs, apply the same logic as individual stocks, Zakat on the zakatable net assets proportional to your share. For money market funds, sukuk funds, and murabaha funds (instruments that are essentially cash equivalents), Zakat is two and a half percent on the total value after one hawl. If you cannot get the breakdown from your fund manager, the conservative position is two and a half percent on total market value.
₿
Cryptocurrency
Emerging consensus: Treated as trade goods, two and a half percent on market value at Zakat date
Contemporary scholars have largely reached consensus that cryptocurrencies held as an investment are analogous to urud al-tijarah (trade goods). As such, Zakat = two and a half percent on the market value at the end of the hawl. The volatile nature of crypto raises a practical question: use the value on your Zakat date consistently each year. Do not time Zakat payment to minimize the value. Crypto held primarily for transactional use follows money Zakat rules.
Retirement Accounts (GOSI, Pension, 401k equivalent)
Scholarly disagreement, two valid positions
Position 1 (Al-Qaradawi, majority): Zakat is due on retirement funds annually, as you constructively own these assets. Pay two and a half percent on the zakatable portion each year.
Position 2 (Ibn Baz, others): Zakat is only due upon actual receipt, i.e., when you receive your pension or GOSI lump sum. Pay Zakat on the full amount received at that time.

Both positions are valid. Pick one and be consistent. If your employer matches contributions you haven't yet received, the conservative view is to include the vested portion in your Zakat base.

Important: Personal Use Items

Your primary residence, personal vehicle, furniture, and clothing are exempt from Zakat regardless of their value. Zakat is levied on productive or liquid wealth, assets held for growth or trade, not personal use. A second property held for investment, however, follows the rental income rule (Zakat on rental income, not property value).

Practical Steps for the Modern Muslim Investor

1. Set a fixed Zakat date. Choose one date per year, most scholars recommend 1 Ramadan for its spiritual significance and because Islamic financial institutions calculate their own Zakat then. Stick to the same date annually.

2. Request the Zakat disclosure from your broker. Saudi brokers on Tadawul and many GCC platforms now publish a per-share Zakat value for listed companies. Al Rajhi Capital and other Shariah-compliant platforms often include a Zakat calculator. Use these rather than estimating.

3. Keep a Zakat ledger. A simple spreadsheet noting each asset, its value on Zakat date, the Nisab check, and the calculation. Useful for your own accountability and if you ever wish to review your methodology with a scholar.

4. When in doubt, consult a scholar or Zakat authority. The General Authority of Zakat and Tax (GAZT) in Saudi Arabia publishes guides for corporate Zakat. For personal questions, scholars affiliated with AAOIFI and the Islamic Fiqh Academy respond to Zakat queries. Uncertainty resolved is better than uncertainty avoided.

Disclaimer: This article is general information, not a fatwa or financial advice. Rulings on zakat can vary by case; consult qualified scholars and a licensed advisor for your specific portfolio.

How is zakat calculated on investment portfolios?

For liquid investments like stocks, ETFs, and mutual funds, zakat is two and a half percent of the market value after one lunar year. For stocks, there are two methods: the "net assets" method (zakat on liquid assets only) or the "simplified" method (two and a half percent of total portfolio value). Most contemporary scholars recommend the simplified method for individuals.

Do I pay zakat on retirement accounts?

Yes, if you have access to the funds. For employer-sponsored retirement plans (like US 401k or UK pension), zakat is due on the vested balance you can access. For self-managed retirement accounts, zakat is due annually on the total value. Some scholars advise paying zakat on the entire balance if you have control over withdrawals.

How do I calculate zakat on real estate investments?

Real estate held for investment (not personal residence) is treated differently. For rental properties, zakat is paid on the net rental income after expenses, at two and a half percent. For properties held for resale (business inventory), zakat is paid on the market value. For personal residence, no zakat is due on the property itself.

What about zakat on gold ETFs and digital gold?

Gold ETFs and digital gold are treated the same as physical gold for zakat purposes. Zakat is two and a half percent of the market value if the gold meets the nisab threshold (approximately 85g of gold, roughly SAR 23,000 or USD 6,200 in 2026). This applies regardless of whether you hold physical gold, gold ETFs, or gold savings accounts.

Can I pay zakat in installments throughout the year?

Yes, paying zakat in installments throughout the year is permissible and often practical for investment portfolios. Calculate the total zakat due annually, then divide by 12 for monthly payments. Some financial institutions offer automated zakat calculators and payment services. The key is ensuring the total amount is correct at year-end.

Practical Tips for Zakat Investment Portfolios

Zakat on a diversified investment portfolio, stocks, REITs, sukuk, mutual funds, is calculated per asset class rather than as one lump sum, since each type follows slightly different rules. Actively traded stocks are zakated at full market value; sukuk, being debt-like instruments, are generally zakated at their redemption or market value.

REITs (real estate investment trusts) are typically treated like company shares for zakat purposes: zakatable on the underlying zakatable assets, mainly cash and receivables, rather than the property holdings themselves, since real estate held for rental income is not itself a zakatable asset the way trading inventory would be.

If your portfolio mixes zakatable and non-zakatable assets, retirement accounts locked until a certain age are a common gray area, keep a simple annual list separating what you can access freely from what is locked, and consult a scholar familiar with modern finance if the split isn't obvious.
