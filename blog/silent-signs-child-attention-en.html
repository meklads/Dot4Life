7 Silent Signs Your Child Needs More Attention (and What to Do)

Children rarely say "I need more attention" in words. Instead, they speak through behaviour. The child who suddenly cannot sleep, who gets stomach aches before school, who becomes excessively quiet or excessively loud — all of these are messages written in a language parents must learn to decode. This guide will help you recognize seven of the quietest, most overlooked signs that your child is craving your presence, and what to do when you see them.

"The child most in need of love will ask for it in the most unloving ways." — Anonymous parenting wisdom

Sign 1: Sudden Regression in Behaviour

What it looks like: A 7-year-old who has been dry at night for years suddenly starts bedwetting. A 5-year-old who speaks in full sentences starts baby-talking. A teenager who dressed independently now wants you to pick their clothes.

What the child may be feeling: Regression is almost always a response to stress or change — a new sibling, starting school, parental conflict, or simply a parent who has been unusually busy. The child is saying: "I need you to take care of me like you used to."

What to do: Do not punish or shame. "You're a big boy, stop acting like a baby" is the worst response. Instead, increase one-on-one time. Read together before bed. Let them sit in your lap even if they are "too old." The regression will fade when the child feels secure again.

Sign 2: Excessive Screen Time or Withdrawal

What it looks like: Your child spends hours on tablets, phones, or TV. When you try to take the device away, they melt down. They prefer screens to playing outside, to family meals, to talking.

What the child may be feeling: Screens are a substitute for connection. A child who withdraws into devices is often a child who has learned that the screen is the only thing that does not reject them. It is a symptom of loneliness, not of laziness.

What to do: Do not just confiscate the device — replace it with something better. Schedule device-free family time that is genuinely fun. Go to the park together. Cook together. Play a board game. The child will choose real connection over screens when real connection is reliably available.

Sign 3: Physical Complaints with No Medical Cause

What it looks like: "My tummy hurts" before school every morning. Headaches that disappear on weekends. Leg pain that comes only at bedtime. You have been to the doctor — nothing is wrong physically.

What the child may be feeling: These are somatic symptoms — physical expressions of emotional distress. The child may be anxious about school, about social dynamics, or about being away from you. The body speaks what the child cannot yet put into words.

What to do: Acknowledge the pain without dismissing it: "I can see your tummy hurts. Let us sit together for 10 minutes and see if it feels better." Sometimes a warm drink, a hug, and a calm conversation is the medicine they need. If symptoms persist, consider a child psychologist.

Sign 4: Acting Out to Get a Reaction

What it looks like: Talking back, breaking rules on purpose, being loud and disruptive, picking fights with siblings. Any reaction from you — even angry shouting — is the goal.

What the child may be feeling: Negative attention is better than no attention. A child who cannot get your warm, focused presence will settle for your anger, because at least they know you see them. This is one of the most heartbreaking truths of child psychology.

What to do: Catch them being good. Give enthusiastic attention for positive behaviour. Ignore minor misbehaviour when safe to do so. And increase your ratio of positive to negative interactions — aim for 5 positive comments for every 1 correction.

Sign 5: The Overly Compliant Child

What it looks like: This is the one that parents often miss, because it looks like good behaviour. The child who never asks for anything, never complains, always follows the rules, always says "yes." They are easy to raise — and they are silently suffering.

What the child may be feeling: "If I am good, maybe they will notice me." Or worse: "If I am good, maybe they will stop being stressed." Some children learn that their needs are a burden, so they stop having needs. This is not mature — it is learned invisibility.

What to do: Check in with this child intentionally. Ask open-ended questions: "What was the best part of your day? The hardest part?" Give them permission to want things, to be messy, to be imperfect. Reassure them that your love is not conditional on their performance.

Sign 6: Sleep Disruptions

What it looks like: Difficulty falling asleep, frequent nightmares, waking up multiple times, resisting bedtime, wanting to sleep in your bed.

What the child may be feeling: Bedtime is separation. For an anxious child, the transition from your presence to being alone in a dark room can feel like abandonment. The child who fights bedtime is often fighting the loss of your company.

What to do: Create a predictable, warm bedtime routine with no screens. Spend 15-20 minutes of focused connection before lights out — reading, talking, praying together. A "worry box" where they write down fears and put them away can help. If they call for you, respond gently but consistently.

Sign 7: Intense Reactions to Small Disappointments

What it looks like: A child who screams because their toast is cut wrong, cries for 30 minutes over a lost game, or has a full meltdown because you said "not right now."

What the child may be feeling: The intensity of the reaction is disproportionate to the trigger — which means the real trigger is not the toast or the game. The child is carrying accumulated emotional weight. The small disappointment is the straw that breaks the camel's back. They are not overreacting — they are telling you they are full.

What to do: Stay calm. Do not match their intensity. Say: "I can see you are really upset. Let me hold you until you feel better." Once they calm down, talk about what is really going on. Often, the true source of distress has nothing to do with the trigger.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much attention does a child need each day?

Quality matters more than quantity. Research suggests that 15-20 minutes of undivided, focused attention per day — no phones, no TV, no multitasking — can significantly improve a child's emotional security. Some children need more, especially during times of transition or stress.

My child shows several of these signs. Should I be worried?

Not necessarily alarmed, but it is a signal to pay closer attention. Start with increasing connection time. If signs persist or worsen over several weeks, consider consulting a child psychologist or family counsellor. Early intervention is always more effective.

What if I cannot give more attention due to work or other children?

Be honest with your child: "I wish I could be with you more right now. Let us make a special 15-minute date every day." Use what you have — a working parent can give focused attention during the commute, during dinner prep, or before bed. The key is consistency, not volume.
