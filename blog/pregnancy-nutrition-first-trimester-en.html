Pregnancy Nutrition: First Trimester

2026-06-088 min read

The First 12 Weeks Shape Everything

The first trimester is your baby's foundational building period: the brain, spinal cord, heart, liver, and kidneys all begin forming within the first 12 weeks , often before many women even realize they're pregnant. What you eat now has a direct impact on your baby's long-term development, even though morning sickness, fatigue, and food aversions make this the hardest time to eat well.

Your actual calorie needs in the first trimester barely increase , only about 150–200 extra calories per day (roughly one and a half bananas). The focus should be on nutrient density, not quantity. Specific vitamins and minerals play critical roles in early organ development, and getting enough of them is what truly matters.

The Non-Negotiable: Folic Acid

Folic acid (400–600 mcg daily) is the one supplement all global health authorities agree on without exception. It reduces the risk of neural tube defects (such as spina bifida) by 50–70%. Ideally, start taking it three months before conception , but if you haven't, start immediately regardless of your current week of pregnancy.

Natural sources include dark leafy greens (spinach, arugula, parsley), beans and lentils, avocado, and citrus fruits. However, a supplement is essential because the required amount exceeds what you can realistically get from food alone. Pair folic acid with iron-rich foods , iron deficiency anaemia affects an estimated 30–40% of pregnant women in the Gulf according to Ministry of Health studies , and maintain adequate hydration in the heat. Iron, Vitamin D, and Calcium: Critical Nutrients

Beyond folic acid, three nutrients deserve special attention during the first trimester:

Iron: Your blood volume increases by nearly 50% during pregnancy, and iron is essential for hemoglobin production. Iron deficiency causes fatigue and increases the risk of preterm birth. Good sources include lean red meat, liver (in moderation), spinach, lentils, and dried fruits. Pair iron with vitamin C (lemon juice, oranges) to improve absorption.

Vitamin D: Deficiency is extremely common in the Gulf, with estimates suggesting 60–80% of women have below-recommended levels due to limited sun exposure and long hours indoors. Vitamin D is crucial for calcium absorption and fetal bone development. Ask your doctor to test your levels and determine the right supplement dosage, especially during the long summer months.

Calcium: The fetus draws calcium from your bones to build its skeleton. Your requirement rises to 1,000–1,300 mg daily. Excellent sources: milk, yogurt, laban (buttermilk), pasteurized cheese, dates (a good source of calcium, potassium, and magnesium), and almonds.

Omega-3 (DHA) for Brain Development

DHA, an omega-3 fatty acid, is a primary building block for your baby's developing brain and retina. While it accumulates most rapidly in the second and third trimesters, your body needs adequate stores from the first trimester onward. Safe Gulf-region sources include salmon, sardines, and mackerel (well-cooked, twice weekly), chia seeds, and walnuts. Purified fish oil supplements are available with your doctor's advice. Avoid high-mercury fish such as shark, king mackerel, and swordfish.

Foods to Approach With Care

Foods to avoid or limit during the first trimester:

  • Raw or undercooked meat and eggs: Risk of toxoplasmosis and listeriosis. Cook meat to an internal temperature of 71°C.
  • Unpasteurised dairy: Soft unpasteurised cheeses (such as some feta or halloumi) may carry Listeria bacteria.
  • High-mercury fish: Shark, king mackerel, swordfish. Limit white (albacore) tuna to one small can per week.
  • Caffeine: Keep under 200 mg daily (about two small cups of coffee or four cups of tea). Saudi/Gulf coffee is typically lower in caffeine, but be mindful of added ingredients.
  • Unverified herbs: Some traditional herbal preparations may stimulate uterine contractions , consult your doctor before any herbal drink.

Dates , a cornerstone of Gulf culture , are safe and highly beneficial in the first trimester: a great source of natural energy, fiber, potassium, and magnesium, especially when nausea limits full meals. Dates do not induce early labour; that is a myth.

Eating Through Nausea

Morning sickness affects 70–85% of pregnant women in the first trimester, and despite the name, it can occur at any time of day. Practical strategies for eating well despite nausea:

  • Small, frequent meals: 6–8 small meals daily are better than 3 large ones. An empty stomach worsens nausea.
  • Dry snack before getting up: Keep plain crackers, toast, or dates by your bedside. Eat them 10 minutes before getting out of bed.
  • Fluids between meals, not with them: Sip water throughout the day, but avoid drinking large amounts with meals to prevent fullness.
  • Cool or room-temperature foods: When cooking smells are overwhelming, cold foods are easier to tolerate because they produce less aroma.
  • Ginger and mint: Mild ginger tea or fresh mint can help settle your stomach. Avoid concentrated ginger supplements.

If you cannot keep any fluids down for 24 hours, feel severely dizzy, or notice very dark urine, contact your doctor immediately , it may be hyperemesis gravidarum, which requires medical intervention.

Recommended Supplements in the First Trimester

Beyond folic acid, your doctor may recommend additional supplements based on your individual needs:

  • Iron: Preventive dose 30 mg daily. Therapeutic dose (for anaemia) 60–120 mg depending on severity. Take with vitamin C (orange juice) to improve absorption. Avoid taking with calcium, tea, or coffee (they reduce absorption).
  • Vitamin D: Common dose 1,000–2,000 IU daily depending on your blood test. In the Gulf, higher doses (up to 4,000 IU) are common due to widespread deficiency affecting 60–80% of women.
  • Calcium: 500–600 mg supplement if your diet does not meet requirements. Best split into two doses (morning and evening) for better absorption.
  • Omega-3 (DHA): 200–300 mg daily, especially if you do not eat fish regularly. Essential for fetal brain development.
  • Iodine: 150 mcg daily , important for thyroid function and fetal nervous system development. Included in most complete prenatal vitamins.

Do not start any supplement without consulting your doctor. Complete prenatal vitamins available at Gulf pharmacies typically contain the appropriate doses of these nutrients together.

Traditional Gulf Foods in the First Trimester

In Gulf culture, several traditional foods are beneficial during the first trimester, while others should be consumed in moderation:

  • Dates (Tamar): Safe and beneficial , excellent source of natural energy, fiber, potassium, magnesium, and iron. They do NOT cause early labor. 3–5 dates daily is a great option, especially during nausea.
  • Milk, Laban, and Yogurt: Excellent sources of calcium and protein. Choose low-fat options if concerned about weight gain. Laban (buttermilk) aids digestion.
  • Traditional soups: Lentil soup (rich in iron and folate), vegetable soup, chicken soup. Light on the stomach and suitable during nausea.
  • Grilled fish: Hamour, sardines, and shaari , excellent sources of omega-3 and protein. Ensure thorough cooking.
  • Herbs with caution: Mint, ginger, and aniseed are safe in moderate amounts. Avoid large amounts of sage and mugwort as they may stimulate uterine contractions. Consult your doctor before any unfamiliar herb.

Traditional foods to limit: Very salty foods (pickles, some salty cheeses) increase fluid retention and blood pressure. Fried foods increase nausea and heartburn. Arabic coffee and tea in moderate amounts (1–2 cups) are within the caffeine limit.

FAQ: Pregnancy Nutrition First Trimester

Do I need to eat for two?
No. You only need about 150–200 extra calories per day in the first trimester. "Eating for two" is a myth that leads to excessive weight gain. Nutrient quality matters far more than quantity.

What if I have severe food aversions?
Food aversions are very common in the first trimester. Focus on what you can tolerate and don't pressure yourself. Prenatal supplements (folic acid, iron, vitamin D) cover the basics. Aversions typically improve by the second trimester.

Are diet sodas safe?
Artificial sweeteners in moderate amounts are generally considered safe, but water or milk are better choices. Regular sodas contain added sugar that increases the risk of gestational diabetes and unhealthy weight gain.

How much weight should I gain in the first trimester?
The recommended gain is only 0.5–2 kg, depending on your pre-pregnancy weight. Most pregnancy weight gain happens in the second and third trimesters.

Conclusion

The first trimester is the nutritional foundation: folic acid, iron, vitamin D, calcium, and omega-3 DHA are the key players. You don't need large quantities , you need nutrient-dense food with targeted supplements. Ignore the "eating for two" myth, listen to your hunger cues, and be kind to yourself through the nausea. Frequently Asked Questions

What are the key nutrients in the first trimester?
Folic acid, iron, calcium and protein are among the most important, alongside plenty of vegetables, fruit, whole grains and regular water intake.

How do I cope with nausea and low appetite?
Eat small frequent meals and plain dry foods like crackers, avoid strong smells, and sip fluids between meals rather than with them.

Which foods should I avoid?
High-mercury fish, raw meat and eggs, unpasteurised cheeses and excess caffeine, to reduce the risk of foodborne infection.

Do I need supplements?
Folic acid is advised before and during the first trimester, and any other supplement should be taken at a dose your doctor sets for your case.
