How to Build a Family Reading Habit

Jun 29, 2026 10 min read

How to Build a Family Reading Habit: A Practical Guide for Gulf Homes

In a region where summer temperatures push past forty-five degrees and screens occupy every corner of the home, the idea of gathering the family around a book can feel like a relic from another era. Yet for a growing number of Gulf parents, building a family reading habit has become one of the most intentional and rewarding decisions they have made.

This guide is written for those parents. It is not a lecture about why reading is important , you already know that. It is a practical, step-by-step playbook for establishing a reading routine in your home, choosing the right children's books, navigating screen-time competition, making use of local libraries in Riyadh, Dubai, and Doha, and sustaining the habit long after the first burst of enthusiasm fades.

One sentence summary: Start with twenty minutes a day, make books visible and accessible, model reading yourself, and build a weekly library visit into your family rhythm.

Why Reading Matters More Now Than Ever

According to WHO, According to WHO, the case for reading has never been stronger, and the threats to it have never been more aggressive. A 2023 report from the UAE's Ministry of Education found that only 38% of children in the country read for pleasure outside of school at least once a week. Similar patterns appear across Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Kuwait. The culprit is not a lack of interest in stories , it is the sheer gravitational pull of YouTube, TikTok, and gaming platforms engineered for maximum retention.

Yet the evidence for reading's impact is overwhelming. Children who read for pleasure score significantly higher on standardised tests in reading, mathematics, and even science, according to a landmark study from the Institute of Education at University College London (Sullivan & Brown, 2015). Beyond academics, regular reading builds empathy, expands vocabulary, improves concentration, and reduces stress. For Muslim families in the Gulf, reading also opens the door to stories of prophets, companions, and Islamic history that strengthen a child's identity and connection to their faith.

But the most compelling reason may be the simplest: reading together is warm, quiet time that a family shares without a screen between them. In a culture that prizes family closeness, reading is one of the most natural ways to protect it.

The Screen Competition

Let us name the elephant in the living room. Your children's devices are designed by some of the brightest engineers in the world to keep their attention as long as possible. Every notification, auto-play video, and infinite scroll is optimised to defeat your best intentions. Competing head-on , by banning screens outright or turning reading into a chore , will not work.

The strategy is not elimination but replacement. Instead of fighting screens, you make reading more appealing. This means:

  • Keeping books where screens normally live , on the coffee table, beside the bed, in the car.
  • Letting children see you reading, not just telling them to read. Modelling is the single most effective parenting tool for building a reading habit.
  • Starting a screen-free window every evening, ideally the hour before bed. During that hour, the family reads together. No phones, no tablets, no television.
  • Using screens strategically , audiobooks on road trips, e-book apps for reluctant readers, reading-based games that reward progress.

The goal is not to eliminate technology from your home. It is to carve out protected time for the printed page, and to make that time feel like a gift rather than a penalty.

Start Small: 20 Minutes a Day

The single most common mistake parents make is aiming too high. Telling yourself your family will read for an hour every evening is a recipe for quitting within a week. The habit is not built by duration; it is built by consistency.

Twenty minutes a day is enough. Set a timer if you need to. What matters is that those twenty minutes happen at roughly the same time each day , right after Maghrib prayer, for example, or just before bedtime. The brain learns to expect it, and the resistance drops.

Here is a simple progression for families starting from zero:

Adjust based on your children's ages and attention spans. The principle is the same: start where you are, not where you wish you were.

Different ages need different approaches. What works for a toddler will frustrate a teenager, and vice versa. Here is a breakdown of what to expect and what to offer at each stage.

The most important rule across every age group: do not turn reading into a test. Do not ask for book reports. Do not insist they finish a book they hate. Reading for pleasure stops being pleasurable the moment it becomes homework.

Creating a Home Library on a Budget

You do not need floor-to-ceiling shelving or a separate room to create a meaningful home library. A single shelf, a basket, or even a stack of books on a side table is enough , provided the books are chosen with care and rotated regularly.

Here is how to build a family library without spending a fortune:

  • Borrow before you buy. Public libraries in Gulf cities are better than most people realise. The Mohammed Bin Rashid Library in Dubai has over a million books, including a dedicated children's section with Arabic and English titles. The Qatar National Library in Doha offers free memberships and a vast digital collection. King Fahad National Library in Riyadh has an excellent children's wing with regular story-time sessions. Use these resources before spending your own money.
  • Buy used. Online marketplaces in the Gulf , including Facebook Marketplace, Dubizzle, and local Telegram groups , often have parents selling children's book collections at a fraction of retail prices.
  • Visit book fairs. The Sharjah International Book Fair (November) and the Riyadh International Book Fair (October) are the two biggest events in the region. Both offer steep discounts, and Sharjah's fair is the world's third-largest, with hundreds of publishers from the Arab world and beyond.
  • Rotate, do not hoard. Keep 20, 30 books accessible at any time. Swap them out every few weeks from a stored collection or from library visits. Children engage more with a limited, rotating selection than with an overwhelming wall of books.
  • Include both languages. If your family speaks Arabic at home but your children attend an English-medium school, provide books in both languages. Biliteracy is a gift that opens doors, and the Gulf is one of the best places in the world to cultivate it.

Quick tip: A book is never wasted even if it is only read once. The cost of a new hardback is less than a single fast-food meal for a family of four, and its impact lasts far longer.

Making Reading a Family Ritual

A habit is something you do. A ritual is something you look forward to. The difference is everything.

To elevate reading from habit to ritual, add small elements of warmth and ceremony:

  • Choose a consistent time and place. The same corner of the sofa. The same cushions. The same dim lamp. The brain begins to relax the moment you enter that space.
  • Start with a calming transition. A few deep breaths, a short dua, or a quiet minute of gratitude before opening the book. This helps children shift from the high-stimulation of screens to the quiet focus of reading.
  • Make it cosy. A cup of warm milk or herbal tea for older children. Soft lighting. Maybe a small fan or air conditioner set to a comfortable temperature , in Gulf summers, physical comfort is not a luxury, it is necessary for focus.
  • Involve every family member. Younger children listen to the read-aloud. Older children read their own books nearby. Parents read too. The point is that everyone is together, each engaged with a book, sharing the same quiet space.
  • Celebrate milestones. When a child finishes their first chapter book, acknowledge it. A simple "masha'Allah" and a new book to look forward to carries more weight than any reward chart.

One Gulf mother from Jeddah shared with us: "We started reading ten minutes after Isha prayer. At first the kids complained. After three weeks, they started reminding me if I forgot. Now it is the most peaceful part of our day , no arguments, no requests for YouTube, just us and our books."

Choosing Books for Muslim Children: Islamic Values and Good Literature

One of the most common questions Gulf parents ask is how to find books that align with Islamic values without being dry, preachy, or poorly written. It is a legitimate concern. The market is full of books that are strong on religious content but weak on storytelling craft , and children sense the difference immediately.

The goal is not to fill your shelves solely with Islamic books. It is to ensure that the books in your home reflect and reinforce the values you are raising your children with, while also being genuinely engaging. Here is what to look for:

  • Illustrated stories of the prophets and companions that emphasise the human qualities , courage, patience, kindness, honesty , that children can relate to and emulate. The best of these use rich illustrations and narrative arcs rather than simple moral lessons.
  • Books about Islamic manners and daily life , how to greet, how to share, how to show gratitude, the duas for everyday moments. These work best when they are embedded in a story rather than presented as a list.
  • Nature and science books that highlight Allah's creation. Books about the human body, the solar system, animals, and the natural world can all be framed with an awareness of the Creator. The wonder of creation is, in itself, a spiritual experience for a child.
  • Historical fiction set in the Islamic Golden Age , stories of Baghdad under Harun al-Rashid, of Cordoba's libraries, of Ibn Sina and Ibn Battuta. These give children heroes from their own civilisation and a sense of pride in their heritage.
  • Well-translated international classics that carry universal values , courage, justice, love, sacrifice , even if they are not explicitly Islamic. A child who reads The Secret Garden or Charlotte's Web in Arabic or English absorbs the same moral lessons that any good story teaches.

The key is balance. A shelf that includes Islamic titles alongside adventure stories, animal tales, science books, and poetry gives a child a rich literary diet. The Islamic content should feel like a natural part of their world, not a separate category they are required to consume.

The Read-Aloud Effect

There is a common misconception that once a child can read independently, parents should stop reading aloud. Research says the opposite. The read-aloud remains valuable well into the teenage years, though the format changes.

When you read aloud to a child, you are doing several things at once: you model fluent reading with expression and pacing, you introduce vocabulary above their current reading level, you create a shared experience around a story, and you communicate , wordlessly , that reading matters enough for you to give it your time and voice.

For younger children, read-aloud time is the foundation of literacy. For older children, it can be a shared novel that you and your child take turns reading aloud, or an audiobook you listen to together during car rides. The shared story becomes a reference point , "remember when that character did X?" , that strengthens your relationship.

The practical advice is simple: keep reading aloud to your children, even after they no longer need you to. Stop when they ask you to, not before.

Using Local Libraries in the Gulf

Gulf cities have invested heavily in public library infrastructure over the past decade. These are no longer dusty, underfunded buildings , they are modern, air-conditioned spaces with dedicated children's wings, digital resources, and regular programming. They are also free or very low-cost. Here is what is available in three key cities:

Riyadh , King Fahad National Library

One of the largest libraries in the Arab world, with a dedicated children's section that holds over 50,000 titles in Arabic and English. The library runs weekly story-time sessions, summer reading programmes, and workshops on everything from creative writing to calligraphy. Membership is free for Saudi residents. The children's wing is designed to be inviting , low shelves, colourful furniture, and a calm, screen-free atmosphere.

Dubai , Mohammed Bin Rashid Library

Opened in 2022, this nine-storey landmark on Dubai Creek is the largest library in the UAE. The children's section spans an entire floor and includes a storytelling amphitheatre, interactive learning stations, and over 100,000 books in multiple languages. The library also has a dedicated Young Adult section and a digital lending platform that allows you to borrow e-books and audiobooks from home.

Doha , Qatar National Library

Qatar National Library is widely regarded as one of the best public libraries in the Middle East. Its Children's Library has over 30,000 books, regular craft sessions, reading challenges, and a dedicated Teen Library. The library's digital collection includes thousands of Arabic and English e-books accessible from anywhere. Membership is free for all residents of Qatar.

Beyond these three, most Gulf cities have excellent local libraries. In Abu Dhabi, the Cultural Foundation Library has a strong children's department. In Kuwait City, the National Library of Kuwait offers story-time and summer programmes. In Manama, the Isa Cultural Centre has a well-stocked children's library. The first step is to find the library nearest to your home and pay it a visit this weekend.

A note for summer: During the hottest months, public libraries are one of the few free, air-conditioned, screen-free spaces where families can spend time together. Make it a weekly summer outing.

FAQ: Building a Family Reading Habit in the Gulf

How do I start a reading habit with a child who hates sitting still?

Start with interactive books , lift-the-flap, touch-and-feel, or pop-up books for younger children. For older kids, try graphic novels or audiobooks during car rides. The key is making reading a low-pressure, enjoyable experience rather than a forced lesson. Let them move around while they listen , some children actually focus better when they are in motion.

How many minutes a day should children read by age?

Ages 2, 5: 10, 15 minutes of parent-led reading. Ages 6, 9: 15, 20 minutes independently or with a parent. Ages 10, 12: 20, 30 minutes. Ages 13+: 30+ minutes. Consistency matters far more than duration. Twenty minutes every day builds a much stronger habit than an hour once a week.

Where can Gulf families find Arabic children's books?

Public libraries in Riyadh (King Fahad National Library), Dubai (Mohammed Bin Rashid Library), and Doha (Qatar National Library) have extensive Arabic children's collections. Online stores like Jamalon ship across the Gulf with reasonable rates. The Sharjah International Book Fair and local book fairs in your city are excellent places to discover new Arabic titles. Many publishers also sell directly through Instagram in the Gulf region.

How do I reduce screen time and replace it with reading?

Start with a screen-free hour before bed that includes family reading time. Keep books visible around the house , on the coffee table, in the kitchen, in the car. Let children see you reading, not just scrolling. Replace one device-based activity at a time rather than banning screens all at once, which tends to backfire. Use a visual timer so children know when screen time ends and reading time begins.

What types of books are appropriate for Muslim children that also teach Islamic values?

Look for illustrated stories of prophets and companions, books about Islamic manners and daily duas, nature and science books that highlight Allah's creation, and age-appropriate historical fiction set in the Islamic Golden Age. The key is balancing value-based content with genuinely engaging storytelling. A good Islamic children's book should first be a good story; the values should emerge naturally from the narrative rather than being tacked on as a lesson at the end.
