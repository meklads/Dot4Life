Hijri New Year: Teaching Children About the Hijra and the Islamic Calendar

Today is 1 Muharram 1448 - the beginning of a new Hijri year. But how many of our children know the meaning of this occasion? With the Gregorian calendar dominating daily life and digital apps, the Hijri year can pass by without children noticing. This guide helps you teach your child the meaning of the Hijra and the Islamic calendar in a way that suits every age.

Why Teach the Hijri Calendar?

The Hijri calendar is not just dates - it's identity and memory. When your child knows we are in the year 1448, they realize their nation has its own calendar, derived from the greatest event in its history: the Hijra. The spread of the Gregorian calendar in screens, schools, and official documents has made the Hijri year seem like a purely religious occasion, while it is the chronology of an entire civilization. Teaching it to children instills pride in Islamic identity and connects them to the story of sacrifice and patience embodied by the Prophet's migration.

The Story of the Hijra for Children - Simply Told

The summary: Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and his companions left Makkah by Allah's command and went to Madinah, because the Quraysh were harming them and preventing them from spreading Islam. This journey became the beginning of the Islamic calendar.

You can tell the story as follows:

  • Prophet Muhammad ﷺ lived in Makkah and loved his people.
  • Some people in Quraysh rejected his message and harmed him and his companions.
  • Allah permitted His Prophet to migrate to Madinah, where the people welcomed him joyfully.
  • The journey was full of events: staying in the Cave of Thawr, the pursuers searching for him, arriving at Quba and building the first mosque.
  • After the Hijra, Muslims had a state and strength, and a new history for the nation began.
  • This is why Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab started the calendar from this blessed year.

How to Explain the Hijri Year by Age

Age GroupAppropriate Explanation
3–6 yearsSimple story: "The Prophet traveled from Makkah to Madinah with his friend on a long journey. They arrived and people were happy." Use simple drawings or toys.
7–10 yearsDetailed telling of the Hijra with events of the Cave of Thawr, names of companions, and feelings of fear and reassurance. Connect to the calendar: "The Hijri year began with a courageous journey."
11–14 yearsDiscuss reasons for the Hijra, political challenges, lessons of trust in Allah, and careful planning. Compare Hijri and Gregorian calendars - why do we need both?
15+ yearsAnalyze the purposes of the Hijra, its civilizational impact, its relationship to contemporary identity, and preserving the Hijri calendar in the age of globalization.

Family Activities for the Hijri New Year

Turn the occasion into a hands-on experience for your children:

  • Hijri Wall Calendar: Print a Hijri calendar of all 12 months. Hang it in the living room and teach your child to track the daily Hijri date.
  • Hijra Map: Draw the Prophet's route from Makkah to Madinah on a simple map. Add key points: Cave of Thawr, Quba, the Prophet's Mosque.
  • Hijri Month Cards: Make cards for each month (Muharram, Safar, Rabi' al-Awwal…) with the meaning of its name and an important event. Play a sorting game.
  • New Year Journal: Ask each child to write or draw a goal for the new Hijri year: "I will memorize this surah," "I will pray Fajr on time."
  • Food Linked to the Hijra: Prepare simple food that connects to the story - Madinah dates, simple meals that remind of Quba hospitality.

Why Are the Hijri Months Named This Way?

Most Hijri month names relate to seasons or customs of ancient Arabs: Muharram (forbidden to fight), Safar (houses emptied for war), Rabi' al-Awwal and Rabi' al-Thani (spring), Jumada al-Ula and Jumada al-Akhirah (dryness), Rajab, Sha'ban, Ramadan (reverence), Shawwal, Dhu al-Qi'dah, Dhu al-Hijjah (pilgrimage months).

The Hijri Calendar Today - Renewed Importance

Today, 1 Muharram 1448, is more than just a new year. In an age dominated by Gregorian dates, returning your child to their original calendar reconnects them with their roots. The Hijri year is not an alternative to the Gregorian - both are important. The Gregorian for practical life, the Hijri for identity and spirit. When your child knows that today is 1 Muharram 1448, they are not just learning a date - they are belonging to a nation spanning 14 centuries of civilization.

Studies (such as reports by Muslim Pro) indicate that 67% of Muslim families in the West primarily use the Gregorian calendar and only engage children with Hijri occasions during Ramadan and Hajj season. This means entire months pass without children understanding their significance. Reviving the Hijri calendar starts at home - with simple rituals that make children feel these dates belong to them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is it called the Hijri calendar?▼

Named after the Hijra (migration) of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ from Makkah to Madinah in 622 CE. This event transformed Islam from a call to a state.

Who established the Hijri calendar?▼

Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab established it in 17 AH, consulting the Companions, starting from Muharram of the year of the Hijra.

How do you convert Gregorian to Hijri?▼

Approximately: Hijri year = Gregorian year - 622 + ((Gregorian - 622) ÷ 32). The Hijri year has 354 days, about 11 days shorter than Gregorian.

What's the difference between Hijri and Gregorian calendars?▼

Hijri is lunar (354 days) tied to the Prophet's migration. Gregorian is solar (365 days) tied to Christ's birth. The 11-day difference means Hijri months move through seasons every 33 years.

Are there special acts of worship in Muharram for children?▼

Fasting Ashura (10th Muharram) is a confirmed Sunnah, recommended with the 9th. Children can fast half a day or help prepare iftar - building love for Islamic traditions early.
